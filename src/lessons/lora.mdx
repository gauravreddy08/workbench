Fine-tuning is taking a model that already knows a lot and training it a little more so it gets good at one particular job, and the trick here is to leave the whole original model untouched and train a small, slim add-on next to it instead.

That trick decides whether you can do the job on your Spark at all. Retraining every number in an 8-billion-number model the ordinary way needs about as much memory as the whole machine has, before it even starts. The add-on version fits with room to spare, saves as a file small enough to email, and once you fold it back in, the model runs exactly as fast as before. That is how you would teach an 8B model to judge query-page relevance for your ranking work in a strict, machine-readable format, without it opening with "Sure! Here's my assessment:" one time in twenty.

It builds on [How much memory does a model need?](/lessons/memory-math/) and [Why training needs so much memory](/lessons/training-memory/), which give you the memory bill this trick dodges, and on [Quantization](/lessons/quantization/) for squeezing the frozen model into fewer bits. If you did [Train your own draft model](/lessons/draft-model/), this is a much cheaper way to run that same kind of retraining. Next, [Why are evals separate from training?](/lessons/why-evals-are-separate/) is about trusting the score you get, and the add-on comes back in [DPO](/lessons/dpo/) and [GRPO](/lessons/grpo/), the cheap way to train models from preferences and rewards on one machine.

## The Problem

Your cross-encoder ranks documents well, but you want something it can't do: a model that reads a query and a page and returns a strict, machine-readable judgement, `{"label": 2, "reason": "..."}`, for labelling training data or for a second-stage check. You try prompting Llama 3.1 8B Instruct. It is right most of the time. It also, about one time in twenty, opens with "Sure! Here's my assessment:", forgets a field, or invents a label 4 on a 0-3 scale. Your parser falls over, at 3 a.m., on the one batch that mattered.

The obvious fix is to train the model on a few thousand examples of exactly the output you want. That's <Term w="fine-tuning">Taking a model that is already trained (pretrained) and training it a bit more on a smaller, specific dataset so it gets good at one job.</Term>. So you do the arithmetic from the training-memory lesson. Training with Adam in mixed precision costs about **16 bytes per parameter** before you store a single activation. 8 billion parameters × 16 bytes = **128 GB**. The Spark has 128 GB of memory in total (spec-sheet number), shared with the operating system and the CPU. It doesn't fit. Not "fits badly": doesn't fit.

And suppose it did. Every task you fine-tune for gives you a new 16 GB copy of the model. A relevance judge, an extractor, a query rewriter: three copies, 48 GB of disk, and if you want to serve all three you need three models in memory.

Here's the strange thing. The change fine-tuning makes to the weights turns out to be tiny and simple. You're paying to update 8 billion numbers when the change you need could often be written down with a few million.

<Origin year="2021" title="Fine-tuning GPT-3 without copying GPT-3">

By 2020, the recipe was "pretrain one big model, fine-tune a copy for each task". That breaks at GPT-3's size: 175 billion parameters is 350 GB in fp16, and a fine-tuned copy per customer is absurd to store, let alone train. There were cheaper alternatives. <Term w="adapters">Small extra layers (a bottleneck MLP) inserted inside each transformer block and trained while the rest is frozen. Houlsby et al., 2019.</Term> trained small new layers inserted between existing ones, but those layers sit in the path of every token and add latency at inference. <Term w="prefix tuning">Training a few "virtual tokens" of key/value vectors that are prepended at every layer, instead of changing weights. Li and Liang, 2021.</Term> learned a handful of fake tokens to prepend, but those eat into the context window and were finicky to optimise.

In 2021 Edward Hu and colleagues at Microsoft proposed something simpler: leave every weight frozen, and learn the *change* to each weight matrix as the product of two thin matrices. On GPT-3 175B, LoRA cut the number of trainable parameters by about 10,000×, cut training memory by about 3×, and shrank a task checkpoint from 350 GB to about 35 MB, while matching full fine-tuning on their benchmarks. And because the two thin matrices can be multiplied out and added back into the weight, it costs nothing extra at inference. That last property is why it won.

</Origin>

## The Idea

### Fine-tuning is a small edit to a big matrix

Strip a transformer down and most of its parameters live in a handful of big <Term w="linear layer">A layer that computes y = Wx (plus an optional bias): multiply the input vector by a weight matrix. In PyTorch, nn.Linear.</Term>s per block: the attention projections `q_proj`, `k_proj`, `v_proj`, `o_proj`, and the MLP's `gate_proj`, `up_proj`, `down_proj`. Each is a matrix `W`, and the layer computes `h = W x`.

Fine-tuning starts from the pretrained `W` and nudges it. After training you have a new matrix, which you can always write as the old one plus a difference:

```text
W_new = W + ΔW
```

Full fine-tuning learns `ΔW` with no restrictions: every one of its entries is free. For a 4096 × 4096 projection that's 16.8 million numbers, for one matrix, in one layer. LoRA's whole idea is to put a restriction on the *shape* of `ΔW` so it can be stored and trained with far fewer numbers.

### The bill for doing it the full way

Before the trick, the cost of not using it. When you train every weight with <Term w="Adam">The standard optimizer for transformers. For every parameter it keeps two running averages: of the gradient (momentum) and of the squared gradient (to scale each step). AdamW is Adam with decoupled weight decay.</Term> in <Term w="mixed precision">Running the forward and backward passes in a 16-bit format (bf16) for speed, while keeping a 32-bit "master" copy of the weights so tiny updates don't round away.</Term>, every parameter carries:

| What | Bytes per parameter |
| --- | --- |
| bf16 weight (used in forward/backward) | 2 |
| bf16 gradient | 2 |
| fp32 master weight | 4 |
| Adam first moment (fp32) | 4 |
| Adam second moment (fp32) | 4 |
| **Total, before activations** | **16** |

Implementations differ a little (some keep fp32 gradients, some drop the bf16 copy), so treat 16 as the standard round number; [Why training needs so much memory](/lessons/training-memory/) takes it apart. On top of this you need <Term w="activations">The intermediate outputs of every layer from the forward pass, kept in memory because the backward pass needs them to compute gradients. Grows with batch size × sequence length.</Term>, which grow with batch size times sequence length.

Now look at what a frozen weight costs. If a parameter doesn't train, it needs no gradient, no master copy and no optimizer state. It costs just its 2 bf16 bytes, or about half a byte if you store it in 4 bits. So the game is: freeze almost everything, and make the part that trains tiny.

### The low-rank bet

Here is the restriction. Instead of learning `ΔW` (shape `d × d`) directly, learn two thin matrices and multiply them:

```text
ΔW = B · A        B is d × r,  A is r × d,  and r is small (8, 16, 64)
```

Think of what `B · A` does to an input `x`. First `A` squeezes the `d`-dimensional `x` down to just `r` numbers. Then `B` spreads those `r` numbers back out to `d` dimensions. Whatever the update does, it has to pass through an `r`-number bottleneck. So the update can only push the output in `r` independent directions. That is exactly what the <Term w="rank">The number of independent directions a matrix can output. A d × d matrix has rank at most d. A product B·A with an r-wide middle has rank at most r, however big d is.</Term> of a matrix means, and why `B · A` is called a <Term w="low-rank">A matrix whose rank is much smaller than its size: it can be written exactly as a product of a tall-thin and a short-wide matrix.</Term> update.

Count the numbers. `B` has `d × r`, `A` has `r × d`, so together `2dr`. Against `d²` for the full matrix:

| d | r | full ΔW (d²) | LoRA (2dr) | fraction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4096 | 8 | 16,777,216 | 65,536 | 0.39% |
| 4096 | 16 | 16,777,216 | 131,072 | 0.78% |
| 4096 | 64 | 16,777,216 | 524,288 | 3.1% |

For a non-square matrix (input size `k`, output size `d`, like Llama's MLP at 4096 → 14336) the same logic gives `r × (d + k)`. Summed over every linear layer of Llama 3.1 8B at `r = 16`, that's **41,943,040** trainable parameters, about 0.52% of the model. Only `q_proj` and `v_proj` at `r = 16`: 6.8 million, under 0.1%.

The bet is that the update fine-tuning *needs* is close to low-rank, so this restriction costs little. Whether that's true depends on the update. Play with it.

Three things to notice while you drag the slider.

1. **The shapes.** At `r = 4`, `B` and `A` are slivers next to `W`, and their combined count is a third of `d²` even at this toy size. At `d = 4096` the same `r` is 0.2%. The savings grow with `d`, because `d²` grows faster than `2dr`.
2. **The error curve's shape is the whole story.** If the update has a few strong directions, the error falls off a cliff at the right rank and then sits at zero. Rank beyond that is wasted. If the update has energy spread over many directions, the curve is a slow slope, and any small rank leaves most of it on the table.
3. **The spectrum bars.** The <Term w="singular values">For any matrix, the strengths of its independent directions, sorted largest first (from the SVD). Rank = how many are non-zero. Keeping the top r gives the best rank-r approximation (the Eckart-Young theorem).</Term> are those strengths, sorted. LoRA can only ever capture the top `r` of them (the blue ones). Everything grey is gone.

### Why a tiny rank is often enough

The deeper reason comes from a 2020 paper by Armen Aghajanyan, Luke Zettlemoyer and Sonal Gupta, "Intrinsic Dimensionality Explains the Effectiveness of Language Model Fine-Tuning". They asked: how many free numbers do you actually need to fine-tune a pretrained model to good accuracy?

Their method: freeze the model, pick a random way to map a small vector of `k` numbers to a change in *all* the weights (a fixed random projection), and train only those `k` numbers. Then find the smallest `k` that still gets you 90% of full fine-tuning's accuracy. That `k` is the task's <Term w="intrinsic dimension">The smallest number of free parameters that, mapped into the full weight space, can still solve the task to a set fraction (here 90%) of full fine-tuning's quality. Li et al. 2018 introduced the measurement.</Term>.

The numbers were startling. For a RoBERTa model on a paraphrase task (MRPC), about 200 trainable numbers, randomly projected into all of its hundreds of millions of weights, got 90% of full fine-tuning's performance. And bigger, better-pretrained models had *smaller* intrinsic dimension: pretraining makes the model so ready for language tasks that adapting it is a short walk in a few directions.

LoRA turns that into an engineering choice: instead of a random projection, a structured one (low rank, per matrix) that is cheap to train and can be multiplied out at the end.

Three design details hide in that line, and each one is there for a reason.

**`B` starts at zero.** With `B = 0`, the side path outputs zero, so `h = W x` exactly: on step 0 the model *is* the base model, with all its abilities. Training starts from a known-good point and moves away only as far as the data pushes it. `A` is initialised randomly (the paper used a Gaussian; Hugging Face's PEFT library uses the same Kaiming-uniform init as `nn.Linear`). Why not both zero? Look at the gradients. The gradient for `B` is proportional to `A x`, and the gradient for `A` is proportional to `Bᵀ` times the incoming gradient. If both start at zero, both gradients are zero, forever, and nothing ever trains. One of them must be random so the other gets a signal. You'll see this exact behaviour in Break It: at step 0, `A`'s gradient is exactly zero and `B`'s is not.

**The scale `α / r`.** `α` (alpha) is a fixed number you pick, usually `r` or `2r`. The point of dividing by `r` is to keep the size of the update roughly steady as you change `r`, so that you can try rank 8 and rank 32 without retuning the learning rate from scratch. The LoRA paper simply set `α` to the first `r` they tried and never tuned it. A later analysis, <Term w="rsLoRA">Rank-stabilised LoRA (Kalajdzievski, 2023): scale by α/√r instead of α/r, which keeps learning stable at large ranks. A flag in PEFT (use_rslora).</Term>, argued that `α / √r` behaves better at large ranks, and PEFT supports both. In practice: fix `α` relative to `r` (say `α = 2r`), and tune the learning rate.

**<Term w="LoRA dropout">Dropout applied to the input of the A·B side path only (not to the frozen path), to regularise the adapter. Typical values 0 to 0.1.</Term>.** Optional dropout on the input to the side path, usually 0.05 to 0.1, as a mild guard against overfitting small datasets.

<Predict id="step-time">
LoRA trains 0.5% of the parameters. Does a training step take 0.5% of the time of full fine-tuning? Roughly what fraction of the compute does it take?
<Fragment slot="answer">

No. Roughly two thirds, and sometimes barely less than full fine-tuning.

A training step for a transformer costs about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token: 2 for the forward pass, 2 for the backward pass to get the gradient with respect to each layer's *input* (needed so the gradient can keep flowing to earlier layers), and 2 to get the gradient with respect to each layer's *weights*. Freezing `W` saves only the last part. The forward pass still runs through every frozen weight, and the backward pass still has to go back through every frozen layer, because the adapters in layer 1 need the gradient that flows back from layer 32. So LoRA is about 4 FLOPs per parameter per token instead of 6, plus a little for the adapters themselves. The big wins are memory (no gradients or Adam states for frozen weights) and storage, not FLOPs. QLoRA is usually *slower* per step than bf16 LoRA, because every frozen weight is unpacked from 4 bits on the fly in both the forward and backward passes.

</Predict>

### Which matrices to adapt

You choose which linear layers get a LoRA wrapper. The LoRA paper, short on memory for GPT-3, adapted only the query and value projections (`W_q`, `W_v`), and found that spreading a fixed budget over both beat putting it all in one. That became the default in a lot of tutorials.

It's not the best default anymore. QLoRA found that adapting **every linear layer**, attention and MLP, was needed to match full fine-tuning quality, and that once you do, the rank matters much less. The "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less" study found the same, and Thinking Machines' 2025 write-up "LoRA Without Regret" found that MLP layers matter most: attention-only LoRA underperformed even at matched parameter count. Most of a transformer's parameters live in the MLP, so that's not surprising.

Practical default: target all linear layers in the blocks (<Term w="PEFT">Hugging Face's library for parameter-efficient fine-tuning: LoRA and its relatives, wrapped around Transformers models.</Term> accepts `target_modules="all-linear"`), leave the embeddings and the output head frozen, and start at `r = 16`.

### Merging: an adapter that costs nothing to run

After training you have `W` (frozen, unchanged) and a small `B, A` per adapted layer. You can serve that as is, computing the side path on every token. Or you can multiply it out once:

```text
W_merged = W + (α / r) · B · A
```

`W_merged` has exactly the shape of `W`. The model is now an ordinary model, same architecture, same speed, nothing extra on the forward path. That's the property that made LoRA beat adapters: **zero added latency at inference**, because there's nothing left to add. PEFT calls it `merge_and_unload()`. You can also go back: subtract the same product and you have the base model again (up to rounding).

Merging has one trap with QLoRA. You trained the adapter against a 4-bit copy of the base. If you merge into the 4-bit weights, the sum has to be re-quantized and much of your tiny update rounds away. The usual practice is to load the base in bf16, merge the adapter into that, and save a bf16 model. That model is very slightly different from the one you trained (bf16 base instead of 4-bit), so always run your eval on the merged model, not just the training checkpoint.

### QLoRA: freeze the base in 4 bits

LoRA got rid of the gradients and optimizer states for frozen weights. The frozen weights themselves are still 2 bytes each: 16 GB for 8B, 130 GB for 65B. In 2023 Tim Dettmers, Artidoro Pagnoni, Ari Holtzman and Luke Zettlemoyer asked: the base is frozen, so why store it in 16 bits at all? <Term w="QLoRA">Quantized LoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023): store the frozen base in 4-bit NF4, dequantize each weight to bf16 on the fly for the matmul, and train bf16 LoRA adapters on top. Fine-tuned a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU.</Term> fine-tuned a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU, and matched 16-bit fine-tuning quality on their benchmarks. Three pieces made it work.

**NF4.** From [Quantization](/lessons/quantization/) you know a 4-bit format has 16 levels and the question is where to put them. Pretrained weights are roughly bell-shaped (normal) around zero. <Term w="NF4">4-bit NormalFloat. The 16 levels are placed at quantiles of a normal distribution, so each level is used about equally often by normally distributed weights. Each block of 64 weights has its own scale (its absolute max).</Term> places the 16 levels at the quantiles of a normal distribution, so each level gets used by roughly the same number of weights: dense near zero where most weights live, sparse in the tails. Each block of 64 weights gets its own scale. For the matmul, the block is unpacked to bf16 on the fly; the adapter math is in bf16 throughout. Gradients flow *through* the 4-bit weights (to reach earlier layers) but never update them.

**Double quantization.** Every block of 64 weights stores one fp32 scale: 32 bits / 64 weights = 0.5 extra bits per weight. That's 4 GB for a 65B model, just for scales. QLoRA quantizes the scales too: to 8 bits, in blocks of 256 scales, each with its own fp32 second-level scale. Cost per weight becomes 8/64 + 32/(64 × 256) ≈ 0.127 bits, saving about 0.37 bits per weight. About 3 GB on a 65B model.

**Paged optimizers.** Memory use during training spikes, for example on an unusually long batch, and a spike at the wrong moment is an out-of-memory crash. QLoRA put the optimizer states in NVIDIA <Term w="unified memory">CUDA memory that can automatically move pages between GPU and CPU memory as they're touched, like an OS swapping pages to disk.</Term>, so on a spike they get paged out to CPU RAM and paged back when needed, like the OS swapping from [How does memory really work?](/lessons/virtual-memory/). On the Spark, CPU and GPU already share one pool of memory, so there's less to page between, but the same option protects you from a crash.

The cost: every frozen weight is <Term w="dequantized">Unpacked from its stored low-bit form back to a normal float (here bf16), using its block's scale, just before the matmul that needs it.</Term> on every forward and backward pass, so QLoRA steps are slower than bf16 LoRA. On the Spark, bf16 LoRA on an 8B fits easily; QLoRA is a choice until you try a 70B.

### What LoRA can and can't learn

Here's the honest part, and it decides when to reach for LoRA at all.

In 2024, Dan Biderman, Jacob Portes, Jose Javier Gonzalez Ortiz and colleagues (Columbia and Databricks) ran the careful comparison in "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less". They trained Llama-2-7B on code and on math, in two regimes: <Term w="continued pretraining">Training further on large amounts of raw text from a new domain (billions of tokens), to teach the model knowledge, not just a format.</Term> (billions of unlabeled tokens) and <Term w="instruction fine-tuning">Training on prompt-response pairs so the model learns to follow instructions or a task format. Usually thousands to a few hundred thousand examples.</Term> (prompt-response pairs). They compared full fine-tuning with LoRA at various ranks. What they found:

- **LoRA learns less.** On the target domain, LoRA substantially underperformed full fine-tuning, most of all in continued pretraining on code. It was much closer in instruction fine-tuning. The full fine-tuning updates had high rank, 10-100× more than typical LoRA settings. A low-rank adapter can't hold a domain's worth of new facts.
- **LoRA forgets less.** Measured on what the base model could already do (language understanding and common-sense benchmarks such as HellaSwag, ARC and WinoGrande), LoRA kept more of it than full fine-tuning did. A small edit breaks less.
- Their advice: target all modules, use a higher learning rate than full fine-tuning, and use LoRA where the goal is adapting behaviour, not absorbing a corpus.

That matches Figure 002. A new output format, a style, a narrow classification or extraction task: "few directions", and LoRA is close to full fine-tuning. Everything about your company's internal APIs, or a new language: "many directions", and LoRA leaves much of it out. For knowledge, retrieval (facts in the prompt) usually beats fine-tuning anyway. Thinking Machines' "LoRA Without Regret" (2025) refines the boundary: with all layers adapted and the right learning rate, LoRA matched full fine-tuning on small-to-medium post-training datasets, and fell behind once the dataset carried more information than the adapter had capacity for.

The flip side, forgetting, has a name. <Term w="catastrophic forgetting">When training on a new task overwrites what a network knew before, so its ability on old tasks drops sharply. Named by McCloskey and Cohen (1989) for small networks; still very real for LLMs.</Term> is what happens when your fine-tune gets great at relevance labels and quietly gets worse at everything else: it stops following other instructions, its general knowledge slips, it answers every question with a relevance label. Lower rank, fewer steps, a lower learning rate, and mixing a little general instruction data into your training set all reduce it. And you only know how much you lost if you measure it, which is why the eval below has two halves.

### Data: chat templates and loss masking

The model learns from tokens, so the most common fine-tuning bugs are about tokens, not math.

**Use the model's own <Term w="chat template">The exact text layout, including special marker tokens, that an instruct model was trained to see conversations in. Stored with the tokenizer and applied by tokenizer.apply_chat_template.</Term>.** An instruct model was trained to see conversations wrapped in specific special tokens. For Llama 3 a user turn looks like `<|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|>`, a blank line, the text, then `<|eot_id|>`. Other model families use other markers. If you train on raw "Query: ... Answer: ..." text and then serve through the chat endpoint (which applies the template), the model sees a format at inference that it never saw in training. Always build training examples with `tokenizer.apply_chat_template(...)`, and serve the same way.

**Mask the prompt out of the loss.** In a training example, the prompt (system message, query, a long document) might be 500 tokens and the answer 15. If you train on every token, 97% of the gradient goes into teaching the model to predict *your prompts*, which it doesn't need to generate. You want loss only on the answer. In Hugging Face models you do that with the labels: copy the input token IDs into `labels`, then set every prompt position to `-100`. The cross-entropy loss ignores any position labelled `-100` (`ignore_index` in PyTorch's `F.cross_entropy`). Keep the end-of-turn token *in* the loss, or the model never learns to stop.

### Hyperparameters, and how overfitting shows up

The knobs, with starting points that are widely used, not laws:

| Knob | Starting point | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learning rate | 1e-4 to 2e-4 for LoRA (full FT: ~1e-5 to 2e-5) | Adapters start at zero and are tiny; they need bigger steps. "LoRA Without Regret" found the best LoRA rate about 10× the best full fine-tuning rate. |
| Rank `r` | 16 (try 8 and 64) | Narrow tasks saturate early. Watch the eval, not the train loss, when choosing. |
| `α` | `2r` (or `r`) | Fix the ratio and tune the learning rate instead. |
| Targets | all linear layers | MLP layers carry much of the benefit. |
| Epochs | 1-3 over a few thousand examples | Small datasets overfit fast. |
| Schedule | short warmup, then cosine or linear decay | Stability at the start, small steps at the end. |

<Term w="overfitting">When a model fits the training examples better and better while getting worse on new ones: it memorises rather than learns the pattern.</Term> in fine-tuning looks like this:

- **Train loss keeps dropping, held-out loss turns up.** The classic. Evaluate on a held-out set every few dozen steps and keep the checkpoint where held-out loss (or better, your task metric) was best.
- **Train loss near zero.** On a few thousand examples, a loss of 0.01 means the model has memorised them. That's almost never what you want.
- **Outputs copy training examples.** Reasons that repeat word-for-word from training data, or the same phrase on every input.
- **Everything becomes your task.** Ask it "what's the capital of France?" and it returns `{"label": 0, ...}`. That's forgetting showing up as overfitting to the format.

### Evaluating a fine-tune

"The loss went down" is not an evaluation. A proper one has two halves.

**1. Did it learn the task?** A <Term w="held-out set">Examples kept completely out of training (and out of any decisions you tune on), used only to measure. If you tune on it, it stops being held out.</Term> scored with the metric you actually care about: label accuracy and <Term w="macro-F1">F1 score (the balance of precision and recall) computed per class and then averaged, so rare classes count as much as common ones. Plain accuracy can hide a model that always predicts the majority label.</Term> for relevance grades, exact-match and field-level accuracy for extraction, plus "fraction of outputs that parse". Compare against the *base model with your best prompt*: if prompting already gets 95%, a fine-tune that gets 96% may not be worth owning. Split carefully. For ranking data, split **by query**, not by row: if the same query appears in train and test with different documents, the model can learn the query, and your test score lies. ([Why are evals separate from training?](/lessons/why-evals-are-separate/) goes deeper.)

**2. Did it break anything else?** A general-capability check, run identically on base and fine-tune: a small slice of standard benchmarks (EleutherAI's `lm-evaluation-harness` runs MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag and friends) and a fixed set of 30-50 everyday prompts you read side by side. You're looking for drops, not gains.

And check it on the model you'll actually serve: the merged one, through your server, with the same chat template and decoding settings.

## Break It

**1. Initialise both A and B to zero.** In your `LoRALinear`, zero `A` too, and train the toy. The loss never moves, not even a little. Print the gradients: both are exactly zero at every step, because each one's gradient is multiplied by the other. Then do the opposite: initialise `B` randomly too. Training runs, but check 3 from t2 fails: at step 0 the model is no longer the base model, it's the base model plus a random perturbation in every layer, and early training spends its effort undoing damage. Zero `B` is the cheap way to start exactly where you want.

**2. Use the wrong learning rate in both directions.** Train the toy LoRA at `lr = 2e-5` (a typical full fine-tuning rate): after 250 steps it has barely learned the format. Then full fine-tune SmolLM2 at `lr = 2e-4` (a typical LoRA rate): loss spikes or goes to `nan`, or it learns the task and forgets how to say anything else. The same number means different things depending on how many parameters it moves and where they start.

**3. Forget the loss mask.** Set `labels = input_ids` for the whole sequence in the toy (or on your real task). The loss you print is now mostly about predicting your prompt template, which falls fast and looks great. The task metric improves much more slowly per step. Then the inverse bug: mask the end-of-turn token too. The model gets the JSON right and keeps going, generating a second turn or rambling until `max_new_tokens`.

**4. Train in one format, serve in another.** Train the toy on raw text (`"Extract ...: <sentence>\n<answer>"`, no chat template), then evaluate through `apply_chat_template` (which is what your server does). Exact match drops sharply. Nothing is wrong with the weights; the input at serving time just isn't the input the adapter learned to respond to.

**5. Merge into the 4-bit weights.** Take your QLoRA adapter, merge it into the 4-bit model and re-quantize (or simulate: merge in bf16, then round-trip the merged weights through NF4), and run the task eval. Compare with merging into a bf16 base. The update is small next to the 4-bit grid's step size, so a lot of it rounds away, and the metric falls back toward the base model.

## Explain It Back

Answer out loud, in plain words, as if in an interview. Then check.

<details>
<summary>1. What is LoRA, in one minute, with the parameter count?</summary>

Fine-tuning changes each weight matrix `W` to `W + ΔW`. LoRA freezes `W` and restricts `ΔW` to be a product `B · A`, where `B` is `d × r` and `A` is `r × d`, with `r` much smaller than `d` (like 16 vs 4096). The update can only change the output in `r` directions, and it takes `2dr` numbers instead of `d²`: for `d = 4096, r = 16`, 131k instead of 16.8M, under 1%. Only `A` and `B` get gradients and optimizer state, so training memory is the frozen model plus a little. `B` starts at zero so training starts exactly at the base model, the update is scaled by `α/r`, and at the end you can add `B · A` into `W` and serve an ordinary model at full speed.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why can't you full fine-tune an 8B model on a 128 GB machine, and how do LoRA and QLoRA get around it?</summary>

Full fine-tuning with Adam in mixed precision needs about 16 bytes per trained parameter: bf16 weight and gradient (2 + 2), an fp32 master weight (4), and two fp32 Adam moments (4 + 4). 8B × 16 = 128 GB before activations, which is the whole machine. LoRA trains ~0.5% of the parameters, so the 16-byte bill applies only to ~42M of them (under 1 GB), and the frozen 8B costs only its 2-byte bf16 weights (16 GB). QLoRA stores those frozen weights in 4-bit NF4 with double-quantized block scales, about half a byte each, so the base drops to ~5-6 GB, at the cost of slower steps because weights are dequantized on the fly.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. How does this scale: what happens to LoRA's savings, and its quality, as models get bigger and tasks get broader?</summary>

The parameter savings grow with model width: `2dr / d² = 2r/d`, so at fixed `r` the adapter is a smaller fraction of each bigger matrix. Bigger pretrained models also have smaller intrinsic dimension (Aghajanyan et al.), so a fixed small rank goes further on them. Memory still scales with the frozen base (2 or ~0.5 bytes per parameter) plus activations, so the base, not the adapter, is what decides whether it fits; for a 70B, QLoRA is what makes one machine enough. Compute per step does not shrink much: still ~4 FLOPs per parameter per token versus 6. On quality, savings scale the other way with task breadth: for a narrow format or task, small ranks match full fine-tuning; as the task carries more new information (continued pretraining on a domain, big datasets), full fine-tuning's update becomes high-rank and LoRA falls behind ("LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less"). In serving, unmerged adapters scale to thousands per base model (S-LoRA), because each is tens of MB.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Your relevance fine-tune scores 94% on your test set. What else do you check before you trust it?</summary>

That the test set is really held out: split by query, no near-duplicate pages across splits, and I didn't tune hyperparameters on it. That 94% beats the base model with my best prompt by enough to matter, and that the gain isn't just parse rate. Per-class numbers and the confusion matrix, since accuracy hides a model that always predicts the majority label. A general-capability check against the base model, to see what it forgot. And all of that on the merged model served through my real server with the production chat template and decoding settings, because merging (especially from QLoRA) and template mismatches can each change the numbers.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why does B start at zero, and what's the α/r scale for?</summary>

With `B = 0`, `B · A = 0`, so the fine-tuned model starts as exactly the base model and only moves as far as the data pushes it. `A` must be random, because each matrix's gradient is multiplied by the other: if both were zero, both gradients would be zero forever. The `α/r` scale keeps the size of the update roughly constant when you change `r`, so you can change the rank without retuning the learning rate from scratch; in practice you fix `α` as a multiple of `r` and tune the learning rate.

</details>

## Go Deeper

- **Edward J. Hu, Yelong Shen, Phillip Wallis, Zeyuan Allen-Zhu, Yuanzhi Li, Shean Wang, Lu Wang, Weizhu Chen, [LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685)** (2021). The original. Read section 4 (the method, init and scaling) and section 7, where they measure the rank of the learned updates and what directions they amplify.
- **Armen Aghajanyan, Luke Zettlemoyer, Sonal Gupta, [Intrinsic Dimensionality Explains the Effectiveness of Language Model Fine-Tuning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13255)** (2020). The "why it works" paper: a couple of hundred well-chosen numbers can fine-tune a big model, and bigger models need fewer.
- **Tim Dettmers, Artidoro Pagnoni, Ari Holtzman, Luke Zettlemoyer, [QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314)** (2023). NF4, double quantization and paged optimizers, plus the finding that adapting all linear layers matters more than rank.
- **Dan Biderman, Jacob Portes, Jose Javier Gonzalez Ortiz, et al., [LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09673)** (2024). The careful, honest comparison with full fine-tuning on code and math. The spectrum analysis is the best picture of when low rank is not enough.
- **John Schulman and Thinking Machines Lab, "LoRA Without Regret"** (2025, on the Thinking Machines blog). Practical, experiment-driven guidance: all layers, ~10× learning rate, when LoRA matches full fine-tuning and when capacity runs out.
- **Ying Sheng et al., [S-LoRA: Serving Thousands of Concurrent LoRA Adapters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03285)** (2023). How to batch requests across many adapters with paging; read alongside the vLLM docs page on LoRA adapters.
- **Hugging Face PEFT documentation, LoRA section** ([huggingface.co/docs/peft](https://huggingface.co/docs/peft)). The real API for `LoraConfig`, `merge_and_unload`, and QLoRA via `bitsandbytes`; check it against the code shapes above.
