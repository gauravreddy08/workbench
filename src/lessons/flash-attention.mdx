Attention is the step where every word in a text looks at every other word to decide which ones matter to it, and FlashAttention is a way of doing that exact same arithmetic while shuffling far fewer numbers around inside the graphics chip. Same answer, to the last rounding error. Much less memory, and much less waiting.

It matters because long inputs are where your work is heading. A day of agent traces, a whole codebase, a long page your ranker has to read: written the obvious way, attention for 32,000 words needs a scratch table bigger than your Spark's entire 128 GB, and it runs out of memory before it finishes the first layer. With FlashAttention the same job needs a few megabytes. Every serious model server, including the one you are benchmarking against Fireworks, depends on it.

It builds on two things you already have: from [Why ~17 tokens per second?](/lessons/bandwidth-bound/), the habit of asking whether a job is waiting on math or on memory, and from [Why is NumPy 100x faster?](/lessons/cpu-caches/), the trick of working on small pieces that fit in fast memory. It is also your first time writing code that runs directly on the graphics chip. After it, the attention code inside real servers ([Inside vLLM and SGLang](/lessons/serving-engines/)) and the decode-time tricks in [The KV cache, by hand](/lessons/kv-cache/) stop being black boxes.

## The Problem

You want to try your Llama implementation on a long document. 32,000 tokens: one long PDF, a codebase, a day of agent traces. Your attention looks like the version everyone writes first:

```python
s = (q @ k.transpose(-2, -1)) / math.sqrt(d)   # (B, H, N, N)
p = torch.softmax(s, dim=-1)
out = p @ v
```

At 2K tokens it is fine. At 8K it is noticeably slow, and the profiler shows the GPU busy but the math units mostly idle. At 32K the Spark, with 128 GB of memory, dies with an out-of-memory error on the first layer.

Notice what is strange here. Q, K and V at 32K tokens are tiny: 32 heads × 32,768 × 128 × 2 bytes = 268 MB each. The output is the same size. The giant thing, `s`, is an intermediate result that you compute, use once, and throw away. You never wanted it. You only wanted the output. Everything bad about long-context attention comes from writing that intermediate down.

<Origin year="2022" title="Attention that is aware of where its bytes live">

By 2020, everyone knew attention was quadratic in sequence length, and a small industry of approximations grew up to fix it: Reformer (hashing), Linformer (low-rank projections), Performer (random features), and a dozen more. They cut the number of FLOPs, sometimes from N² to N. They mostly did not make training faster in wall-clock time, and they changed the model's math, which made people nervous to use them.

In 2022, Tri Dao, Dan Fu, Stefano Ermon, Atri Rudra and Christopher Ré at Stanford published "FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness." Their argument was that everyone had been counting the wrong thing. On a GPU, attention is not limited by FLOPs; it is limited by moving the N × N matrix in and out of GPU memory. Keep it on the chip and you can do *more* FLOPs and still finish sooner, with the exact same answer as before. Their kernel trained GPT-2 about 3x faster than the standard implementation and made 16K-token sequences practical for the first time. The trick that makes it possible, computing softmax in one streaming pass, came from a 2018 NVIDIA note by Maxim Milakov and Natalia Gimelshein. Within two years, FlashAttention (or something built the same way) was inside essentially every training and serving stack, including PyTorch itself.

</Origin>

## The Idea

### Two kinds of memory on one chip

In the [caches lesson](/lessons/cpu-caches/) you met a CPU's ladder: registers, L1, L2, L3, RAM, each one bigger and slower than the last. A GPU has the same ladder, with one big difference: the fast rungs are tiny and *you* manage the most important one by hand.

A GPU is made of many <Term w="SMs">Streaming multiprocessors: the GPU's "cores", each a block of math units with its own small fast memory. An A100 has 108; the Spark's GB10 has dozens (check yours).</Term>. Each SM has three kinds of storage on it, and the whole chip shares two more.

- <Term w="Registers">The fastest storage there is, inside each SM, private to each thread. A few hundred KB per SM in total. Values here are what the math units actually operate on.</Term>: where the numbers being multiplied right now live.
- <Term w="shared memory" label="shared memory (SRAM)">A small scratchpad on each SM, built from SRAM, that a group of threads fills and reads by hand. Around 100 to 228 KB per SM depending on the chip. Shared by the threads of one block, invisible to other SMs.</Term>, which the FlashAttention paper and most people just call <Term w="SRAM">Static RAM: fast memory built into the chip itself. On a GPU this means registers, shared memory and caches. Fast and tiny because each bit costs several transistors.</Term>: a scratchpad you load tiles into and reuse.
- An L1 cache, which on NVIDIA chips is carved out of the same SRAM as shared memory.
- <Term w="L2">A cache shared by all SMs, tens of MB on data-center GPUs. Managed by the hardware, not by you. If data you re-read is still here, you skip the trip to main memory.</Term>, shared by the whole chip.
- <Term w="HBM">High-bandwidth memory: the GPU's main memory, stacked DRAM next to the chip. Tens of GB, a few TB/s. People say "HBM" for GPU main memory in general; on the Spark it is LPDDR5x shared with the CPU, but it plays the same role.</Term>: the big main memory, where every tensor you allocate in PyTorch lives.

Two numbers to hold onto. On the A100 the FlashAttention paper used, on-chip SRAM moves about 19 TB/s in aggregate and HBM about 1.5 TB/s: roughly a 12x gap. (That SRAM figure is just arithmetic: 108 SMs × 128 bytes per clock × 1.41 GHz ≈ 19.5 TB/s.) On the Spark the gap is wider. Its main memory is 273 GB/s on the spec sheet, while its SRAM, by the same arithmetic with its own SM count and clock, is on the order of 10 TB/s or more. Every byte you avoid sending to main memory is worth even more on your machine than on the A100 the paper was written for.

The programming model follows from the hardware. A <Term w="kernel" label="kernel (GPU)">One function that runs on the GPU, launched from the CPU. Thousands of copies run at once, each on its own slice of the data. Not the operating-system kernel.</Term> is launched as a grid of <Term w="thread blocks">Groups of GPU threads (often 128 or 256) that run together on one SM and can share that SM's shared memory. In Triton, one block is one "program".</Term>. Each block lands on one SM, loads what it needs from HBM into its shared memory and registers, computes, and writes results back to HBM. Blocks cannot see each other's shared memory, and when a kernel ends, everything in SRAM is gone. **The only way to pass data from one kernel to the next is through HBM.** Hold that sentence; the whole lesson hangs off it.

Count the bytes for one head, in elements. Reading Q and K is 2Nd. Writing S is N². Reading S back is N². Writing P is N². Reading P and V is N² + Nd. Writing O is Nd. Total: **4N² + 4Nd**. For N = 8,192 and d = 128, the 4Nd part is 4 million elements and the 4N² part is 268 million. The useful inputs and outputs are 1.5% of the traffic. The rest is S and P, going out and coming back.

That is the minimum. Real eager code is often worse: a separate kernel for the `/ sqrt(d)` scale (read N², write N²), another for a <Term w="causal mask">The rule in a decoder that token i may only attend to tokens at or before i. Implemented by setting the scores for future tokens to minus infinity before the softmax.</Term> (`masked_fill`, read N², write N²), dropout during training (more again). Each elementwise op on S is two more trips.

Now, the roofline from [the bandwidth lesson](/lessons/bandwidth-bound/). The FLOPs for attention, per head, are about 4N²d: 2N²d for Q Kᵀ and 2N²d for P V. Divide by bytes (4N² elements × 2 bytes, ignoring the small part) to get the <Term w="arithmetic intensity">FLOPs done per byte moved to or from main memory. Compare it with the chip's ridge point (peak FLOP/s divided by bandwidth) to know which one limits you.</Term>:

```text
arithmetic intensity (naive)  ≈  4 N² d / (8 N²)  =  d / 2  FLOPs per byte
d = 128  →  64 FLOPs/byte        d = 64  →  32 FLOPs/byte
```

The Spark's ridge point, from the bandwidth lesson, is a few hundred FLOPs per byte (around 460 on spec-sheet numbers). An A100's is around 150 to 200. Naive attention at 64 sits well to the left: <Term w="memory-bound">The work finishes when the data finishes arriving. Faster math would not help; fewer or faster bytes would.</Term>. And notice that the intensity does not depend on N. Longer sequences give you more work *and* proportionally more bytes, so you never climb out.

### The obvious fix, and why it is hard

If S and P only go to HBM because kernels cannot hand data to each other, the fix is to do everything in one kernel: <Term w="kernel fusion">Merging several operations into one GPU kernel so the intermediate results stay in registers or shared memory instead of making a round trip through HBM.</Term>. Load a few rows of Q, compute their scores, softmax them, multiply by V, write the output rows. S and P exist only inside the SM, a tile at a time, and never touch HBM.

For elementwise ops this is easy, and compilers do it automatically. `torch.compile` will happily fuse the scale, the mask and the exponent into one pass. The problem is softmax, because softmax is not elementwise. To output even one probability, you need the sum of exponentials over the **entire row**:

```text
softmax(x)_i  =  e^(x_i) / Σ_j e^(x_j)
```

A row of S has N entries. At N = 32K, one row in fp32 is 128 KB, about the size of an SM's whole shared memory, and a block wants to work on 64 or 128 rows at once. You cannot hold a full row of scores on the chip for long sequences. So it looks like you have to compute the whole row, store it somewhere (HBM), sum it, and come back. That is exactly the round trip we are trying to kill.

The way out is to find a way to compute softmax while seeing the row **one piece at a time**, never holding all of it. That is the online softmax.

### Softmax, the safe way

First, a detail you may have skipped in GPT-2, because `torch.softmax` hid it. You never compute `e^(x_i)` directly. In fp16 the largest number is 65,504, and e^x passes that at x ≈ 11.09. Scores bigger than 11 are normal in attention. In fp32 it overflows at x ≈ 88.7. On my Mac, `np.exp(np.float16(12.0))` is `inf`, and softmax of `[1000, 1001, 1002]` computed directly comes back as `[nan, nan, nan]` (inf divided by inf).

The fix is to subtract the row's maximum first. Softmax does not change when you shift every input by the same constant, because the shift factors out of the top and the bottom:

```text
e^(x_i - m) / Σ_j e^(x_j - m)  =  (e^(x_i) · e^(-m)) / (e^(-m) · Σ_j e^(x_j))  =  softmax(x)_i
```

With m = max(x), every exponent is at most 0, so every e^ is between 0 and 1 and nothing overflows. This is the <Term w="safe softmax">Softmax computed as e^(x - max) / sum of e^(x - max). Mathematically identical to plain softmax, but no exponent is ever positive, so nothing overflows.</Term>, and every real implementation does it. It costs three passes over the row:

1. Pass 1: find m = max of the row.
2. Pass 2: compute l = Σ e^(x_j − m).
3. Pass 3: output e^(x_i − m) / l.

Three reads of every element. For a row in HBM, that is three trips. Milakov and Gimelshein's question in 2018 was: can passes 1 and 2 be one pass?

### Online softmax: keep a running max and a running sum

Here is the scene. Numbers arrive one at a time, like cards dealt from a deck. You keep two things on a sticky note: the biggest number you have seen so far, **m**, and a running sum, **l**, of e^(x − m) for everything so far, *measured against the current m*.

When a new number x arrives, there are two cases.

- **x is not a new max.** m stays. Add e^(x − m) to l. Done.
- **x is a new max.** Every term already in l was computed as e^(x_j − m_old). You want them as e^(x_j − m_new). Since e^(x_j − m_new) = e^(x_j − m_old) · e^(m_old − m_new), you can fix **all of them at once** by multiplying l by one number, e^(m_old − m_new). Then add e^(x − m_new), which is e^0 = 1.

Both cases are the same formula:

```text
m_new = max(m, x)
l_new = l · e^(m - m_new)  +  e^(x - m_new)
start:  m = -∞,  l = 0
```

When the max does not change, e^(m − m_new) = e^0 = 1 and the formula is just "add". When it does change, the factor is less than 1 and shrinks the old sum to the new scale. At the very start, e^(−∞) = 0, so the empty sum vanishes cleanly.

Walk it through on x = [2, 1, 5, 3]:

| Arrives | m | Rescale e^(m_old − m_new) | l |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 2 | 0 (from −∞) | 0 · 0 + e^0 = 1 |
| 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 + e^(−1) = 1.3679 |
| 5 | 5 | e^(2 − 5) = 0.0498 | 1.3679 × 0.0498 + e^0 = 1.0681 |
| 3 | 5 | 1 | 1.0681 + e^(−2) = 1.2034 |

The two-pass way: m = 5, l = e^(−3) + e^(−4) + e^0 + e^(−2) = 0.0498 + 0.0183 + 1 + 0.1353 = 1.2034. Same number.

**Why it is exact, not approximate.** Claim: after seeing the first k numbers, m_k is their max and l_k = Σ over j ≤ k of e^(x_j − m_k). Prove it by induction.

- After 1 number: m_1 = x_1 and l_1 = 0 · e^(−∞) + e^(x_1 − x_1) = 1 = e^(x_1 − m_1). True.
- Suppose it is true after k numbers. Then m_(k+1) = max(m_k, x_(k+1)) is the max of the first k + 1, and
  l_(k+1) = l_k · e^(m_k − m_(k+1)) + e^(x_(k+1) − m_(k+1))
  = Σ_(j ≤ k) e^(x_j − m_k) · e^(m_k − m_(k+1)) + e^(x_(k+1) − m_(k+1))
  = Σ_(j ≤ k) e^(x_j − m_(k+1)) + e^(x_(k+1) − m_(k+1))
  = Σ_(j ≤ k+1) e^(x_j − m_(k+1)). True.

So at the end, l is exactly the safe-softmax denominator. Nothing was dropped or estimated; the only differences from the two-pass version are floating-point rounding. You still need a second pass to produce the outputs e^(x_i − m) / l, but max and sum are now one pass.

Now generalize. Two workers each process half of a row and each ends up with their own sticky note: (m₁, l₁) and (m₂, l₂). How do you combine them into the (m, l) for the whole row, without looking at the numbers again?

<Fragment slot="answer">

m = max(m₁, m₂), and l = l₁ · e^(m₁ − m) + l₂ · e^(m₂ − m). Each partial sum is rescaled to the common max and then they are added. The one-at-a-time update is just the special case where the second worker saw a single number (m₂ = x, l₂ = 1). This merge is associative, so you can split a row into any number of pieces, process them in any grouping, and merge. That single fact powers FlashAttention (merge tile by tile), Flash-Decoding (split the KV cache across many SMs and merge at the end), and ring attention (split a sequence across GPUs).

### From a sum to an output row

Attention does not want the softmax itself. It wants the softmax times V: for one query row, o = Σ_j softmax(s)_j · v_j, a weighted average of value vectors. The beautiful part is that this output can ride along on the same sticky note.

Keep a third running quantity, **o**, a vector of length d: the un-normalized sum Σ e^(s_j − m) · v_j against the current max. When a new score s and its value vector v arrive:

```text
m_new = max(m, s)
α     = e^(m - m_new)                       # the rescale factor
l_new = l · α  +  e^(s - m_new)
o_new = o · α  +  e^(s - m_new) · v          # same rescale, same new weight
```

At the end, the answer is o / l. The proof is the same induction: o is always Σ e^(s_j − m) v_j against the current m, because rescaling by α moves every past term to the new max at once. Dividing by l at the end turns the weights e^(s_j − m) / l into exactly softmax(s)_j.

Now do it a **tile** at a time instead of one key at a time. Take a block of Bc keys and their values. Compute that block's scores for one query row, take the block's max, and apply the update to the whole block at once:

```text
s_blk  = q · K_blkᵀ                          # Bc scores
m_new  = max(m, max(s_blk))
p      = e^(s_blk - m_new)                   # Bc weights
α      = e^(m - m_new)
l      = l · α + sum(p)
o      = o · α + p · V_blk                   # (Bc) @ (Bc × d) → d
m      = m_new
```

That is the merge rule from the Predict, with the block as the "second worker". And it is shaped like a matmul: `p · V_blk` is a small dense matrix product, which is what GPUs are built for.

### FlashAttention's forward pass

Put it together. Give each GPU program a block of Br query rows. The program:

1. Loads its Q block (Br × d) from HBM into SRAM, **once**.
2. Sets m = −∞, l = 0, o = 0 for each of its Br rows. These live in registers.
3. Loops over the K and V blocks, Bc rows at a time. For each: load K_j and V_j into SRAM, compute the Br × Bc score tile S = Q_blk K_jᵀ, update m, l, o with the block rule above.
4. After the last block, divides o by l and writes the Br × d output block to HBM. Also writes one extra number per row, the <Term w="logsumexp">m + log(l) for a row: the log of the softmax denominator. One number per query row. Storing it lets the backward pass rebuild any probability as e^(s - L) without recomputing the sum.</Term> L = m + log(l), for the backward pass.

The score tile S and the probability tile P exist only in SRAM and registers, for one block at a time. Nothing N × N is ever written. Step through it below on a tiny example: 8 keys in blocks of 2, d = 2, and one program's 2 query rows. Watch the α column. Whenever a row's max goes up, its old l and o shrink. At the end, check the output against the exact answer.

### Counting bytes: the IO argument

The paper's real contribution, beyond the trick, was to count. Its title says <Term w="IO-aware">Designed around the cost of moving data between levels of memory (here HBM and SRAM), not only the cost of arithmetic. IO means input/output: reads and writes.</Term>: judge an algorithm by how many bytes it moves between HBM and SRAM, not just by FLOPs.

Per head, the Q-block-outer version moves:

- Q: read once, Nd elements.
- K and V: read once **per query block**, so (N / Br) × 2Nd.
- O: written once, Nd.

Total about 2Nd + 2N²d / Br. Compare with naive's 4N² + 4Nd. The block size Br is limited by SRAM: the Q tile, a K and V tile, and the S tile all have to fit, so Br and Bc end up around M / d for an SRAM of M elements. Substitute and the paper's theorem pops out: FlashAttention needs Θ(N² d² / M) HBM accesses, against Θ(N² + Nd) for standard attention. The paper also proves that no exact attention algorithm can do asymptotically better than that across all SRAM sizes.

Plug in real numbers and you get something more modest than "quadratic to linear". With d = 128 and Br = 128, the worst case is 2N² elements against naive's 4N²: a 2x saving, plus the eager extras. Both are still quadratic in N, which the paper is clear about; the saving is the constant d² / M.

So why is FlashAttention many times faster in practice? Three reasons, in order of how much they matter on current hardware:

1. **K and V are re-read from L2, not HBM.** The worst case assumes every re-read of K and V goes all the way to main memory. But K and V for one head at N = 8K and d = 128 are 4 MB together, and the programs working on the same head run at about the same time. Their re-reads mostly hit in L2. In the best case, real HBM traffic is just Q, K, V and O once: 4Nd, linear in N.
2. **The eager extras vanish.** Scale, mask, dropout and softmax all happen inside the one kernel, on tiles already in registers.
3. **Intensity moves past the ridge.** With traffic of about 4Nd and FLOPs of 4N²d, intensity is about N FLOPs per byte. At N = 8K that is in the thousands, far right of any ridge point. FlashAttention turns attention from a memory-bound job into a compute-bound one, which is why the next generations of the kernel are all about using the math units better.

And then there is memory. Naive's S and P need 2 × H × N² × 2 bytes; flash needs 2 numbers per query row. At N = 32K, that is 137 GB against 8 MB. That difference alone is what made long-context training possible, before any speed argument.

### The backward pass: recompute instead of remember

Training needs gradients. The standard backward pass for attention uses P, the N × N probability matrix, saved from the forward pass. FlashAttention never stored P. What now?

It **recomputes** it. The forward pass saved O (N × d) and the logsumexp L (one number per row). In the backward pass, a kernel walks the same tiles again: reload Q, K, V tiles, recompute S = Q Kᵀ for the tile, and rebuild the exact probabilities as P = e^(S − L). No running max is needed this time, because L already is the final answer to "what is this row's normalizer". With the P tile in hand, the gradients follow from the chain rule, tile by tile:

```text
dV  += Pᵀ dO                      # (Bc × Br) @ (Br × d)
dP   = dO Vᵀ                      # per tile
D    = rowsum(dO ∘ O)             # one number per query row, computed once
dS   = P ∘ (dP − D)               # softmax backward, elementwise
dQ  += dS K · scale       dK += dSᵀ Q · scale
```

The D trick is worth a second look. The softmax backward for row i needs Σ_j P_ij dP_ij, which would mean a full pass over the row. But Σ_j P_ij dP_ij = Σ_j P_ij (dO_i · v_j) = dO_i · (Σ_j P_ij v_j) = dO_i · O_i. You already have O. So D is one dot product per row, done once, before the tile loop.

This is a deliberate trade: **more FLOPs, fewer bytes.** The recomputation adds roughly one extra Q Kᵀ matmul, and the paper's backward pass did more FLOPs than standard attention and still ran faster, because the bytes saved were worth more than the FLOPs spent. You will see this trade everywhere in ML systems once you know to look: <Term w="activation checkpointing">Also called gradient checkpointing: during training, throw away some intermediate results after the forward pass and recompute them during the backward pass. Saves memory at the cost of extra compute.</Term> is the same idea at the level of whole layers.

### FlashAttention-2, -3, and what came after

Once attention is compute-bound, the game changes from "move fewer bytes" to "keep the math units busy." The later versions are mostly about that.

**FlashAttention-2** (Tri Dao, 2023) got about 2x over the first version, reaching 50 to 73% of an A100's peak FLOPs. Three changes:

- **Fewer non-matmul FLOPs.** <Term w="Tensor cores">Special units on NVIDIA GPUs that do only one thing, small matrix multiply-adds, many times faster than the regular math units. Most of a GPU's advertised FLOPs live here.</Term> do matrix multiplies roughly 16x faster than the regular units do exponentials and divisions. So FA-2 keeps o unnormalized until the very end (one division per row, not one per block) and trims every other bit of scalar bookkeeping.
- **Parallelize over the sequence.** FA-1 gave one program to each (batch, head) pair. With batch 1 and long sequences, that can be fewer programs than the GPU has SMs. FA-2 also splits over query blocks, which is the loop order you just stepped through.
- **Better split of work inside a program.** Within a block, FA-2 divides the *queries* among its <Term w="warps">Groups of 32 GPU threads that execute the same instruction together. A thread block is several warps.</Term> instead of the keys, so warps do not have to exchange partial results through shared memory.

It also skips blocks that are fully masked out for causal attention, roughly halving the work when every token can only see the past.

**FlashAttention-3** (Jay Shah, Ganesh Bikshandi, Ying Zhang, Vijay Thakkar, Pradeep Ramani and Tri Dao, 2024) is specific to the H100 (Hopper). It reached about 740 TFLOP/s in fp16, around 75% of peak, and close to 1.2 PFLOP/s in FP8. The ideas:

- **Asynchrony.** Hopper has a hardware copy engine (TMA) and asynchronous tensor-core instructions. FA-3 dedicates some warps to loading tiles and others to math (<Term w="warp specialization">Giving different warps in a block different jobs, for example "producer" warps that only load data and "consumer" warps that only compute, so loading and math overlap.</Term>), so the next tile arrives while the current one is being multiplied.
- **Overlapping softmax with matmul.** The exponentials are slow and the tensor cores sit idle during them. FA-3 staggers two groups of warps so one does its softmax while the other does its matmul ("ping-pong").
- **FP8**, with per-block scaling and a trick to spread out outlier values before quantizing.

FA-3's code relies on Hopper-only instructions. NVIDIA's data-center Blackwell (B200) got its own rewrite, often called FlashAttention-4, in Tri Dao's repository; check the repo for its status. Your Spark's GB10 is a different Blackwell variant from the B200, and as far as I know it does not have the B200's new tensor-core instructions, so neither FA-3 nor FA-4 targets it. Check what is supported for compute capability 12.1 before you assume anything. What *does* work everywhere is the algorithm, and PyTorch's own `scaled_dot_product_attention`, which picks a backend for you.

One more relative: **Flash-Decoding** (Dao and collaborators, 2023). In decoding, there is one query row and a long KV cache. A single query block gives the GPU almost nothing to parallelize, so Flash-Decoding splits the *keys* across many programs, each producing its own (m, l, o), and then merges them with the rule from the Predict above. Same trick, opposite axis.

### Writing a kernel: Triton

You could write all this in CUDA C++, managing threads, warps and shared memory by hand. <Term w="Triton">A Python-embedded language and compiler from OpenAI (Philippe Tillet) for writing GPU kernels. You write code for one block of data; the compiler handles threads, shared memory and much of the scheduling.</Term> lets you think one level up: you write the code for **one program working on one tile**, and the compiler decides how threads inside that program split the work, when to use shared memory, and how to feed the tensor cores.

The key ideas, in a vector add:

```python
import torch, triton
import triton.language as tl

@triton.jit
def add_kernel(x_ptr, y_ptr, out_ptr, n, BLOCK: tl.constexpr):
    pid = tl.program_id(0)                       # which program am I? 0, 1, 2, ...
    offs = pid * BLOCK + tl.arange(0, BLOCK)     # the BLOCK element indices I own
    mask = offs < n                              # the last program may run past the end
    x = tl.load(x_ptr + offs, mask=mask)         # gather a whole tile from HBM
    y = tl.load(y_ptr + offs, mask=mask)
    tl.store(out_ptr + offs, x + y, mask=mask)   # write the tile back

def add(x, y):
    out = torch.empty_like(x)
    grid = (triton.cdiv(x.numel(), 1024),)       # how many programs to launch
    add_kernel[grid](x, y, out, x.numel(), BLOCK=1024)
    return out
```

- **A program** is one instance of the kernel. You launch a *grid* of them, and `tl.program_id(axis)` tells each which one it is. One program runs on one SM (as one thread block); many run at once.
- **Pointers and offsets.** PyTorch tensors arrive as pointers to their first element. `x_ptr + offs` is a whole vector of addresses, and `tl.load` fetches them all in one go. For 2D tiles you build a grid of addresses from row and column offsets with broadcasting: `ptr + rows[:, None] * stride + cols[None, :]`.
- <Term w="mask" label="mask (Triton)">A vector of True/False the same shape as the tile, passed to tl.load and tl.store. Lanes where it is False are not read (they get the `other` value instead) and not written. This is how a kernel handles sizes that are not a multiple of the block.</Term>. N is rarely a multiple of your block size. If N = 300 and BLOCK = 128, the third program owns indices 256 to 383, and only 44 of those exist. Without a mask it reads memory past the end of the tensor (garbage, or a crash); with `mask=offs < n` it skips them. `tl.load(..., mask=m, other=0.0)` says what value to use for the skipped lanes.
- **`tl.constexpr`** marks values known at compile time, like block sizes. Triton compiles a separate version of the kernel for each value, and block sizes must be powers of 2.
- **Tile operations**: `tl.dot(a, b)` multiplies two tiles on the tensor cores (each dimension at least 16), `tl.max(x, axis=1)`, `tl.sum`, `tl.exp`, `tl.where`, `tl.maximum`. Everything works on whole tiles, like NumPy.

### Benchmarking a kernel without lying to yourself

GPU timing has more ways to be wrong than right. The rules:

1. **Synchronize.** As in [Waiting vs working](/lessons/waiting-vs-working/), a kernel launch returns as soon as the work is queued. Time without `torch.cuda.synchronize()` (or CUDA events) and you measure how fast Python can queue work. You will get numbers that beat the hardware's peak.
2. **Warm up.** The first call to a Triton kernel compiles it, which takes hundreds of milliseconds to seconds. The first call to a <Term w="cuBLAS">NVIDIA's library of tuned linear-algebra kernels. When PyTorch multiplies two matrices on the GPU, it usually calls cuBLAS.</Term> matmul may run a heuristic search. PyTorch's memory allocator grabs memory from the driver the first time and reuses it after. Run each function several times before you start the clock.
3. **Repeat and take a robust statistic.** Run many iterations; report the median (or mean, with the spread). GPU clocks change with temperature and power.
4. **Watch the cache.** If you run the same small kernel 100 times in a row, its inputs may sit in L2 and you measure L2 speed, not HBM speed. `triton.testing.do_bench` clears the L2 by writing a big buffer between runs (check the source of your version to confirm).
5. **Check correctness before speed.** A wrong kernel can be extremely fast. Compare against a reference first, with a tolerance that fits the dtype: fp16 attention typically agrees to about 1e-3, not 1e-6.
6. **Time only the kernel.** Allocate inputs outside the timed region. Keep shapes fixed so nothing recompiles.

`triton.testing.do_bench(fn)` does most of this for you: it warms up, synchronizes, flushes L2, runs repeatedly for a target time, and returns milliseconds. (In recent versions the arguments are `warmup` and `rep` in milliseconds and the default statistic is the mean; read the docstring for your version.) For memory, `torch.cuda.reset_peak_memory_stats()` before a call and `torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated()` after tell you the high-water mark of PyTorch's allocations.

<Task id="t3" title="A fused softmax kernel in Triton" agent="hands">

Warm up on a kernel with one row per program. This is softmax over the last dimension of a 2D tensor, in one read and one write of HBM.

```python
# softmax_triton.py
import torch, triton
import triton.language as tl

@triton.jit
def softmax_kernel(out_ptr, in_ptr, row_stride, n_cols, BLOCK: tl.constexpr):
    row = tl.program_id(0)
    cols = tl.arange(0, BLOCK)
    mask = cols < n_cols
    # 1. load this row: in_ptr + row * row_stride + cols, masked, other=-inf
    #    (why -inf and not 0? think about the max and the exp)
    # 2. subtract the max, exponentiate, sum, divide (in float32)
    # 3. store to out_ptr with the same mask
    ...

def softmax(x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    n_rows, n_cols = x.shape
    out = torch.empty_like(x)
    BLOCK = triton.next_power_of_2(n_cols)
    softmax_kernel[(n_rows,)](out, x, x.stride(0), n_cols, BLOCK=BLOCK)
    return out

def softmax_eager(x):
    """The unfused version: several separate kernels, several HBM round trips."""
    m = x.max(dim=1, keepdim=True).values
    e = torch.exp(x - m)
    return e / e.sum(dim=1, keepdim=True)
```

<Task id="t4" title="A FlashAttention forward kernel in Triton" agent="hands">

Now the real thing, simplified: forward pass only, no causal mask, no dropout, fp16 inputs, fp32 accumulators, head dim 64 or 128. One program per (query block, batch × head). The wrapper and the setup are given; the loop body is yours. It is the same loop as your NumPy `tiled_attention`, written with `tl.` functions.

```python
# flash_triton.py
import torch, triton
import triton.language as tl

@triton.jit
def _flash_fwd(Q, K, V, O, N, stride_bh, stride_n, scale,
               BLOCK_M: tl.constexpr, BLOCK_N: tl.constexpr, D: tl.constexpr):
    pid_m = tl.program_id(0)            # which query block
    pid_bh = tl.program_id(1)           # which (batch, head)
    base = pid_bh * stride_bh
    rows = pid_m * BLOCK_M + tl.arange(0, BLOCK_M)
    dims = tl.arange(0, D)
    offs_n = tl.arange(0, BLOCK_N)

    # Q tile: (BLOCK_M, D), loaded once
    q = tl.load(Q + base + rows[:, None] * stride_n + dims[None, :],
                mask=rows[:, None] < N, other=0.0)

    m_i = tl.full([BLOCK_M], float("-inf"), tl.float32)
    l_i = tl.zeros([BLOCK_M], tl.float32)
    acc = tl.zeros([BLOCK_M, D], tl.float32)

    for start in range(0, N, BLOCK_N):
        cols = start + offs_n
        # 1. load K tile TRANSPOSED as (D, BLOCK_N): address = K + base + cols[None, :] * stride_n + dims[:, None]
        #    mask on cols < N, other=0.0
        # 2. s = tl.dot(q, k) * scale; then set columns >= N to -inf with tl.where
        # 3. m_new = max(m_i, row max of s); p = exp(s - m_new[:, None]); alpha = exp(m_i - m_new)
        # 4. l_i = l_i * alpha + row sum of p
        # 5. load V tile (BLOCK_N, D), masked; acc = acc * alpha[:, None] + tl.dot(p.to(v.dtype), v)
        # 6. m_i = m_new
        ...

    acc = acc / l_i[:, None]
    tl.store(O + base + rows[:, None] * stride_n + dims[None, :],
             acc.to(O.dtype.element_ty), mask=rows[:, None] < N)

def flash_fwd(q, k, v, BLOCK_M=64, BLOCK_N=64):
    """q, k, v: (B, H, N, D) fp16 on cuda. Returns (B, H, N, D)."""
    B, H, N, D = q.shape
    q, k, v = (t.reshape(B * H, N, D).contiguous() for t in (q, k, v))
    o = torch.empty_like(q)
    grid = (triton.cdiv(N, BLOCK_M), B * H)
    _flash_fwd[grid](q, k, v, o, N, q.stride(0), q.stride(1), 1.0 / D ** 0.5,
                     BLOCK_M=BLOCK_M, BLOCK_N=BLOCK_N, D=D)
    return o.reshape(B, H, N, D)
```

Hints:

- `tl.max(s, axis=1)` and `tl.sum(p, axis=1)` reduce over the key dimension. `tl.maximum(a, b)` is elementwise max.
- `tl.dot` wants both inputs in the same dtype; `p` is fp32, so cast it to fp16 before the second dot. The accumulator stays fp32.
- Test with `torch.testing.assert_close(flash_fwd(q, k, v), F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v), atol=2e-2, rtol=2e-2)`, for N = 1024, N = 1000 (partial blocks) and N = 77.

<details>
<summary>1. Attention does the same FLOPs whether you write it naively or as FlashAttention. So why is the naive version slow for long sequences?</summary>

Because the naive version is limited by memory traffic, not math. It runs as separate kernels, and kernels can only pass data through HBM, so the N × N score matrix gets written to HBM, read back for softmax, the N × N probability matrix gets written, and read back again for the multiply with V. That is at least 4N² numbers of traffic against only about 4Nd for the real inputs and outputs. The arithmetic intensity comes out to about d / 2 FLOPs per byte, around 64 for d = 128, well below the ridge point of any modern GPU, and it does not improve with N. FlashAttention fuses everything into one kernel and keeps the score tiles on the chip, so it moves roughly the inputs and outputs only and becomes compute-bound. It also never allocates the N × N matrix, which at 32K tokens and 32 heads would be over 60 GB for one layer.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Explain online softmax, and convince me it is exact.</summary>

Keep a running max m and a running sum l of e^(x − m) over everything seen so far. For each new value x: the new max is max(m, x); multiply the old sum by e^(m_old − m_new), which re-expresses every previous term relative to the new max in one step, because e^(x_j − m_new) = e^(x_j − m_old) · e^(m_old − m_new); then add e^(x − m_new). By induction, after k values l is exactly the sum of e^(x_j − m_k) over the first k values, which at the end is the safe-softmax denominator. There is no approximation, only floating-point rounding. Two partial states combine the same way, m = max of the two, each l rescaled to it and added, which is what lets you process tiles independently and merge. For attention you carry a third quantity, the un-normalized output o, rescaled by the same factor, and divide by l at the end.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. How does FlashAttention scale with sequence length, head dimension and SRAM size, in time, memory and HBM traffic?</summary>

FLOPs are still about 4N²d per head for the forward pass, so compute time grows as N²; FlashAttention does not change the asymptotic work. Extra memory drops from N² per head (the score and probability matrices) to N per head (m and l, or the logsumexp), so it is linear. HBM traffic in the paper's model is Θ(N² d² / M), with M the SRAM size, against Θ(N² + Nd) for naive: still quadratic in N, but with a smaller constant that improves as SRAM grows and gets worse as d grows, because bigger heads mean smaller tiles fit. The paper proves no exact algorithm can do better across all M. In practice, K and V re-reads often hit in L2, so real traffic is closer to linear, and the kernel is compute-bound at long N. That is why FlashAttention-2 and -3 focus on keeping the tensor cores busy rather than on moving fewer bytes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does the backward pass recompute attention instead of saving the probabilities?</summary>

Saving P would mean writing an N × N matrix per head to HBM in the forward pass and reading it back in the backward: exactly the memory and traffic FlashAttention was built to avoid. Instead the forward saves the output O and one number per row, the logsumexp L = m + log l. The backward reloads Q, K, V tiles, recomputes the score tile, and rebuilds exact probabilities as e^(S − L), then computes the gradient tiles. The softmax gradient needs a row sum of P times dP, which simplifies to the dot product of dO and O per row, computed once up front. It costs roughly 25% more FLOPs in the backward but saves far more time in memory traffic, and it removes the quadratic memory. Same trade as activation checkpointing: spend cheap compute to avoid expensive bytes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. You are asked to make a new attention variant fast, say one with a per-head learned bias added to the scores and a different normalization. How do you approach it?</summary>

First write the reference in plain PyTorch and a test harness with awkward shapes, so every faster version has something to match. Then ask the FlashAttention question: can the computation be done one key tile at a time with a small running state per query row, and can two partial states be merged exactly? An additive bias that you can compute per tile is easy: add it to the score tile before the max. A different normalization needs its own running statistic and merge rule; if it has one (like softmax's max and sum), it tiles; if it needs the whole row at once, you need two passes or a different algorithm. Then count bytes and FLOPs to know whether the fused kernel should be memory- or compute-bound, write it in Triton starting from a working flash kernel, check exactness, and benchmark properly: warmup, synchronize, do_bench, several sequence lengths, compared against SDPA on the plain-softmax case. Then profile with Nsight Compute to see whether you are near the roofline, and only then tune block sizes and warps.

</details>
