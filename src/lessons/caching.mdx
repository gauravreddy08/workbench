## The Problem

Your eval harness runs 500 prompts through the server on the Spark every time someone changes the ranking code. Temperature 0, the same model, the same prompts. Every run, the GPU spends about 40 minutes re-deriving 500 answers it already gave yesterday, word for word.

So you put a dictionary in front of it. Key: the prompt. Value: the answer. The second run takes 4 seconds. You feel like a genius for about a week.

Then the bugs arrive, one at a time.

You swap in a fine-tuned checkpoint and the eval scores don't move at all. Of course not: the cache is still serving the old model's answers, because the model wasn't part of the key. You fix that, and a teammate's sampling experiment at temperature 0.8 produces exactly one answer per prompt no matter how many times it runs, because temperature wasn't part of the key either. You move the cache to Redis so the three replicas share it, set every entry to expire after an hour, and at 10:00 every morning, an hour after the 9:00 warm-up job, the GPU queue spikes to hundreds of requests for the same handful of prompts, all at once.

Each of these is a classic, with a name and a known fix. A cache is the cheapest speedup in computing and also the most reliable way to serve something wrong with total confidence. This lesson is about both halves.

<Origin year="1965" title="Slave memory, then memcached, then Redis">

In 1965 Maurice Wilkes wrote a two-page paper, "Slave Memories and Dynamic Storage Allocation", proposing a small fast memory that would automatically hold copies of whatever the processor had used recently from the big slow one. IBM shipped the first commercial version in the System/360 Model 85 in 1968 and called it a cache. Every CPU since has one.

The same trick moved up the stack whenever something got slow. In 2003 Brad Fitzpatrick's blogging site LiveJournal was drowning its databases in repeated reads, so he wrote memcached: a big hash table in RAM, spread across spare machines, that any web server could ask before bothering the database. Facebook later ran it at enormous scale and wrote up what broke. In 2009 Salvatore Sanfilippo, building a real-time analytics product called LLOOGG that was too slow on MySQL, wrote Redis: like memcached, but storing lists, sets and other data structures instead of only strings, and able to save itself to disk.

The line everyone quotes is usually credited to Phil Karlton at Netscape: "There are only two hard things in Computer Science: cache invalidation and naming things." Keep it in mind. Making a cache fast is easy. Knowing when its answers stop being true is not.

</Origin>

## The Idea

### A cache is a bet

A <Term w="cache">A small, fast store that keeps copies of results from a bigger, slower source, so repeated requests skip the slow part.</Term> is a bet that you'll ask for the same thing again soon. When you win, a <Term w="hit">A request the cache can answer from its copy. The slow source is never touched.</Term>, you skip the slow work entirely. When you lose, a <Term w="miss">A request the cache can't answer. You pay the full slow cost, plus the small cost of having checked the cache first.</Term>, you do the slow work, and usually store the result in case it's asked again.

Why is that bet usually a good one? You met the CPU version in [How slow is slow?](/lessons/latency-numbers/): programs reuse what they used recently, called <Term w="temporal locality">The tendency to ask for something again soon after asking for it once. The reason "keep what was used recently" is a good rule.</Term>. People do the same. The prompt someone sent a second ago is far more likely to come again than one from last month.

The second reason is stronger, and it's about popularity, not time. Rank every distinct request by how often it's asked. In real traffic (web pages, search queries, words in text, songs, API calls, prompts) the counts are wildly lopsided. The most popular item gets asked twice as often as the second, three times as often as the third, and so on. That shape is a <Term w="Zipf distribution">A popularity curve where the k-th most popular item is requested about 1/k^s as often as the most popular one. With s near 1, a few items get a big share of all traffic and a long tail of items is rarely asked.</Term>, and the exponent `s` (the <Term w="skew">How lopsided popularity is. s = 0 means every item is equally popular; bigger s means the top items take more of the traffic.</Term>) says how lopsided.

Zipf is why small caches work. Here is what I measured on this Mac with a 15-line Python simulation: 10,000 distinct items, 200,000 requests drawn from a Zipf distribution, and an LRU cache (defined properly later) of a given size starting empty.

| Cache holds | s = 0 (uniform) | s = 0.8 | s = 1.0 | s = 1.2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 items (0.1%) | 0.1% | 3% | 13% | 34% |
| 100 items (1%) | 1% | 16% | 39% | 66% |
| 1,000 items (10%) | 10% | 44% | 67% | 86% |
| 5,000 items (50%) | 49% | 78% | 89% | 95% |

Read the s = 1.0 column. A cache holding 1% of the items answers 39% of requests. One holding 10% answers two thirds. Then read the uniform column: with no skew, a cache holding 10% of items answers 10% of requests, and caching is almost pointless. **The workload decides whether a cache helps, not the cache.** Before you build one, look at how often your requests actually repeat.

The CPU caches from [Why is NumPy 100x faster?](/lessons/cpu-caches/) copy RAM. The kernel's page cache from [What happens when you save a file?](/lessons/files-and-disks/) copies the SSD. A Python `dict` or `functools.lru_cache` copies the result of a function. Redis copies the result of a database query or a model call, and is shared by every server process. A <Term w="CDN">Content delivery network. Thousands of servers spread around the world that keep copies of your files (and sometimes API responses) close to users, so most requests never cross an ocean.</Term> copies your web server's responses, near the user. The browser copies them on the user's own disk. And on the GPU, the [KV cache](/lessons/kv-cache/) and [prefix cache](/lessons/prefix-caching/) copy attention keys and values that would otherwise be recomputed.

Every layer faces the same four questions, and the rest of this lesson is about them:

1. **What is the key?** What exactly must match for a stored answer to count as the answer to this request?
2. **How long is a copy allowed to be trusted?** (TTL, invalidation.)
3. **When it's full, what leaves?** (Eviction.)
4. **What happens on a miss, especially many misses at once?** (Stampedes.)

What changes between layers is who answers them. The CPU answers them in silicon, the kernel in C, the CDN with HTTP headers you send. At the application layer, which is today's layer, you answer them, in code, and you get them wrong in the ways from the Problem.

### The arithmetic

Every request either hits or misses. If a fraction `h` of requests hit (the <Term w="hit rate">The fraction of requests answered from the cache. 1 minus the hit rate is the miss rate.</Term>), the average cost is:

```text
effective latency = h · t_hit + (1 − h) · t_miss
```

Plug in your server. A hit through Redis costs about 1 ms. A miss costs about 2,000 ms of GPU time for a short answer.

| Hit rate | Average latency | Speedup vs no cache |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0% | 2,000 ms | 1× |
| 50% | 1,000 ms | 2× |
| 90% | 201 ms | 10× |
| 99% | 21 ms | 95× |

Look at the jump from 90% to 99%. Nine more points of hit rate, ten times faster. That's because the misses dominate the sum: `t_miss` is 2,000 times `t_hit`, so the average is basically the miss rate times the miss cost. **Think in miss rate, not hit rate.** Going from 10% misses to 1% misses is a 10x improvement; going from 90% hits to 99% hits sounds like a rounding error.

Two more consequences of the same formula. A cache whose hit costs almost as much as a miss is useless at any hit rate (caching a 2 ms database query behind a 1 ms Redis round trip saves you at most half). And a cache with a terrible hit rate makes things slower, because every miss also pays the lookup.

<Predict id="p50-p99">

Your server gets traffic with a 70% hit rate. Hits take 1 ms, misses take 2,000 ms. What are the p50 and the p99 latencies, with the cache and without it?

<Fragment slot="answer">

Without the cache, both are about 2,000 ms.

With it, sort all latencies from fastest to slowest. The fastest 70% are hits, all about 1 ms. The median request (the 50th percentile) falls inside that 70%, so **p50 is about 1 ms**, a 2,000× improvement. The 99th percentile falls in the slowest 30%, which are misses, so **p99 is still about 2,000 ms**: no improvement at all.

p99 only drops to hit speed once the miss rate is below 1%. This is the honest summary of most caches: they make the typical request magical and leave the worst requests exactly as bad. If your users suffer from p99 (from [p99 and load testing](/lessons/tail-latency/): with fan-out, they usually do), a cache isn't the fix by itself. There is one indirect win: on your one-at-a-time GPU server, every hit is a request that isn't standing in the queue, so the misses that remain wait less. You'll see that in Measure.

**<Term w="cache-aside">The application checks the cache, and on a miss reads the source itself and stores the result. The cache is a passive key-value store. The most common pattern.</Term>** is what you'll build today, and what most code using Redis does: `get`, and if nothing came back, compute and `set`. The cache knows nothing about where values come from. Pros: simple, the cache can be any key-value store, and if the cache dies your code still works (slowly). Cons: every caller has to remember the dance, and a miss costs three steps.

**<Term w="read-through">The cache itself knows how to load a missing value (you give it a loader function). Callers only ever ask the cache.</Term>** moves the loader into the cache. `functools.lru_cache` is read-through: you call the function, the decorator checks its table and calls the real function on a miss. CDNs are read-through: on a miss the edge fetches from your origin. The win is that loading logic lives in one place, which is also the natural place to make sure only one caller loads a given key at a time (more on that soon).

Those two are about reads. Writes raise a sharper question: when the data changes, who updates the cache?

**<Term w="write-through">Every write updates the cache and the source together, and only then reports success. Reads after a write see the new value.</Term>** keeps them in step: slower writes, fresh reads. **<Term w="write-behind">The cache accepts the write and reports success immediately, then writes to the source later, often in batches. Also called write-back.</Term>** makes writes fast and lumps them together, at the price that a crash can lose writes the caller already thought were saved. Your CPU's caches are write-back, and so is the page cache: in [What happens when you save a file?](/lessons/files-and-disks/) you saw that `write()` returns long before the bytes reach the SSD, which is exactly why `fsync` exists.

For LLM responses there are no "writes" in the usual sense: the source (the model) never changes an answer behind your back; only *you* change the model. So cache-aside with a careful key is the right shape. The write patterns matter the moment you cache something users can edit.

### How long to trust a copy: TTLs

A copy is a snapshot. The source may change after you took it, and the cache has no way of noticing. The bluntest, most reliable answer is to give every entry an expiry date: a <Term w="TTL">Time to live. How long a cache entry may be served after it was stored. After that it counts as missing, and the next request refetches it.</Term>. In Redis, `SET key value EX 3600` stores a value that disappears an hour later.

A TTL is a promise about **staleness**: "this answer is at most an hour out of date." Choosing it is a business question wearing a technical costume. Ask three things:

1. **How wrong can the answer be, and for how long, before someone is hurt?** A stock price: seconds. A product description: an hour. A user's permissions after being fired: zero, which means TTL alone is the wrong tool.
2. **How often does the source actually change?** Data that changes once a day gains nothing from a 10-second TTL except more misses.
3. **What does a miss cost?** If a miss is a 2 ms query, short TTLs are cheap. If it's 2 seconds of GPU, every expiry is expensive, and you want long TTLs plus some other way to handle changes.

For your LLM cache, the answer to (2) is "never, until you change the model or the prompt template". So the right TTL is long (days), and the thing that actually keeps answers correct is the key, not the clock. The TTL is there to bound memory and to clean up keys no one will ever ask for again.

Two refinements you'll use. **Jitter**: instead of exactly 3,600 seconds, use 3,600 plus or minus a random 10%. Otherwise everything you stored in one burst (a warm-up job, a deploy) expires in one burst, which is the 10:00 spike from the Problem. **Negative caching**: also cache "not found" answers, briefly. If a missing user ID is requested 1,000 times a second, without a negative entry every one of those is a miss that hits the database.

### Invalidation, the hard part

The alternative to waiting for a TTL is to remove or update the copy the moment the source changes: <Term w="invalidation">Removing or updating a cached copy because the source data changed. The hard part is doing it at the right moment, everywhere the copy lives, without races.</Term>. With cache-aside, the usual recipe is: write to the database, then delete the cache key. The next read misses and fetches the fresh value. It sounds airtight. It isn't.

<Predict id="invalidate-race">

Two requests, one cache-aside cache, using "write the database, then delete the key". Request R reads key `user:7`, misses, and queries the database. Meanwhile request W updates user 7 in the database and deletes `user:7` from the cache. Describe an ordering of these steps that leaves the cache holding stale data. How long does it stay stale?

<Fragment slot="answer">

1. R misses in the cache and reads user 7 from the database: the **old** value.
2. W writes the new value to the database.
3. W deletes `user:7` from the cache (there's nothing there yet).
4. R, which was slow (a GC pause, a busy thread), now does its `SET user:7 <old value>`.

The cache now holds the old value, and nothing will ever remove it, because the invalidation already happened. It stays wrong until the TTL expires, or forever if there is no TTL. That's why people say "always set a TTL, even if you invalidate": the TTL is the backstop that bounds how long any bug in your invalidation can last.

Real fixes include: have the reader only set if no invalidation happened in between (Facebook's memcache uses short-lived "leases" for this), use versioned keys (below), or delete the key a second time a little later. The point is that once a copy exists in two places, keeping them in agreement is a distributed-systems problem, with races, and not a line of code.

Invalidation is also hard because you have to know every key a change affects. A user renames themselves: which cached pages, search results and recommendation lists contain the old name? You trade memory for correctness, which is almost always the right trade. **For your LLM cache this is the whole strategy: the model, its weights revision and the prompt template version go into the key, so changing any of them changes every key.**

### When it's full: eviction

RAM is finite. When the cache is full and a new entry arrives, something has to leave. Choosing what is <Term w="eviction">Removing an entry to make room for a new one, because the cache is full. Not the same as expiry: an evicted entry may still have been valid.</Term>, and the rule is the <Term w="eviction policy">The rule that picks which entry to evict. LRU, LFU, FIFO and random are the common ones.</Term>. The ideal rule would evict the entry that will be needed furthest in the future (Bélády's optimal algorithm, 1966), which you can't know. Every real policy is a guess about the future from the past.

- **<Term w="LRU">Least recently used. When full, evict the entry whose last use is oldest. Bets that what was used recently will be used again soon.</Term>** bets on recency. Cheap, adapts quickly when popularity shifts, and the default nearly everywhere. Its classic weakness: one big scan (a batch job reading 100,000 keys once each) pushes out every hot entry, because each scanned key is "recently used" for a moment.
- **<Term w="LFU">Least frequently used. When full, evict the entry that has been used the fewest times. Bets on popularity rather than recency.</Term>** bets on frequency. Resists scans (a key used once never outranks a key used a thousand times), but is slow to forget: yesterday's viral prompt keeps its huge count. Practical LFUs let counts decay over time.
- **<Term w="ARC">Adaptive Replacement Cache (Megiddo and Modha, IBM, 2003). Keeps a recency list and a frequency list, plus "ghost" lists of recently evicted keys, and shifts space between the two lists depending on which ghosts get asked for again.</Term>** keeps both kinds of evidence and tunes the balance by watching its own mistakes. Used in ZFS. Modern designs in the same spirit (W-TinyLFU in Java's Caffeine library) admit a new entry only if it looks more popular than the one it would push out.
- **FIFO and random** sound naive and are often within a few points of LRU on real traffic, with less bookkeeping. Redis itself doesn't run exact LRU: it samples a handful of keys and evicts the oldest among them.

Each node stores its key, its value, its expiry time, and two pointers: `prev` and `next`. The map stores key → node. Then:

- **get(k)**: look up the node in the map. Missing: miss. Expired: unlink it, delete it from the map, miss. Otherwise unlink it from where it is and relink it right after HEAD. Return the value.
- **set(k, v)**: if the key exists, update the node and move it to the front. Otherwise create a node, put it at the front, add it to the map, and if the map is now over capacity, take the node just before TAIL, unlink it, and delete its key from the map.

Why *doubly* linked? To unlink a node from the middle of the list you need to change its predecessor's `next`, and with only forward pointers, finding the predecessor is a walk from HEAD. The `prev` pointer makes unlinking O(1). And why does each node store its own key? Because at eviction time you start from the node (just before TAIL) and need to delete the matching map entry.

Notice how TTL fits in: it's checked **lazily**, at `get` time. An expired entry that nobody asks for just sits there until LRU pushes it out. That's exactly what Redis does too, plus a background sweep. `collections.OrderedDict` is this same structure, written in C, with `move_to_end` and `popitem(last=False)`; it's what the [prefix cache](/lessons/prefix-caching/) used. Today you build it by hand once, so it's never magic again. Expect your version to be a few times slower than a plain `dict` lookup: on this Mac a hand-written Python `get` took about 0.15 µs against 0.03 µs for `dict.get`. Both are a rounding error next to a 1 ms network round trip.

The fixes, from simplest to most thorough:

- **<Term w="request coalescing">When several requests for the same key miss at once, only the first one does the work; the rest wait for its result and share it. Go's standard library calls this singleflight.</Term>.** Keep a table of "in-flight" keys. A miss first checks it: if someone's already computing this key, wait for their result instead of starting another. In one asyncio process this is a dict from key to `asyncio.Future`, about ten lines. It only works within one process, though; three replicas still make three calls.
- **A lock in the shared cache.** Across processes or machines, the first miss takes a lock with `SET lock:<key> <id> NX PX 10000` (set only if it doesn't exist, expire in 10 seconds, so a crashed holder can't block everyone forever). Whoever wins recomputes. Losers wait briefly and re-check the cache. More moving parts, but it works across the whole fleet.
- **Jittered TTLs.** Doesn't stop one hot key's stampede, but stops many keys from expiring together, which is what turns small stampedes into one big one.
- **<Term w="stale-while-revalidate">When an entry expires, keep serving the old copy for a while and refresh it in the background. After the soft expiry, the first reader triggers one background refresh and everyone, including that reader, keeps getting the old value until the new one lands. Nobody waits. The cost is serving answers a bit past their TTL, which you've already decided is acceptable if you're caching at all.
- **Probabilistic early refresh.** Each reader, as an entry nears expiry, recomputes early with a small probability that grows as the deadline approaches, so one reader almost always refreshes before the cliff. The "XFetch" paper below shows how to pick the probability.

You'll build coalescing and the lock, and see each one's limit.

### Redis, in one sitting

<Term w="Redis">An in-memory data structure server: a separate program that holds keys mapped to strings, hashes, lists, sets, sorted sets and more, in RAM, and answers commands over a TCP connection. Used as a cache, a queue, a rate limiter, a session store.</Term> is a program you run next to your servers. You connect over TCP and send short text commands: `SET k v EX 60`, `GET k`, `INCR counter`, `ZADD leaderboard 42 alice`. It keeps everything in RAM, so a command is usually a few microseconds of work, and the round trip over the network or loopback dominates (roughly 0.1 ms on one machine; measure it in the Build).

Four things make it what it is.

**It's single-threaded where it matters.** Redis executes commands one at a time on one thread, with an <Term w="event loop">A single loop that runs ready work one piece at a time and, when nothing is ready, sleeps until the OS reports that some socket or timer is ready.</Term> just like the one you built in [Waiting vs working](/lessons/waiting-vs-working/): it asks the kernel (epoll on Linux) which client sockets have commands waiting, runs each command to completion, writes the reply, repeat. Since Redis 6, extra threads can help read and write sockets, but command execution stays on one thread. Two big consequences. Every command is <Term w="atomic">Happens all at once or not at all, with nothing else interleaved. No other client can see a half-done INCR.</Term> for free: `INCR` can't race with another `INCR`, because nothing runs at the same time. And one slow command (`KEYS *` on ten million keys, a huge `SORT`) blocks every other client, exactly like a blocking call in an `async def`. Redis is fast because each command is tiny, not because it does many at once.

**It stores data structures, not just strings.** <Term w="hash" label="Redis hash">A Redis value that is itself a small map of field → value, like a Python dict stored under one key.</Term>es, lists (queues), sets, <Term w="sorted set">A set where each member has a score, kept ordered by score. Leaderboards, priority queues, sliding-window rate limiters.</Term>s, streams (append-only logs), plus counters, bitmaps and HyperLogLog (approximate counting of distinct items). That's why it's used for far more than caching: the [rate limiter](/lessons/rate-limits/) you built could live in Redis and be shared by every replica.

**It expires and evicts.** Any key can carry a TTL. Expired keys are removed lazily (when someone touches one) and by a background job that samples keys with TTLs several times a second. When memory hits the `maxmemory` limit, `maxmemory-policy` decides what happens. The default, `noeviction`, refuses new writes with an error, which is right for a database and wrong for a cache. For a cache you set `allkeys-lru` or `allkeys-lfu`. Redis's LRU is approximate: it samples a few keys (5 by default) and evicts the best candidate among them, which in practice is close to true LRU and saves two pointers per key.

**It can survive a restart, if you ask.** Two <Term w="persistence">Saving in-memory data to disk so it survives a restart. Redis offers snapshots (RDB), an append-only log (AOF), both, or neither.</Term> options, both built on things you've seen. **RDB snapshots**: Redis calls `fork()`; the child process writes the whole dataset to a file while the parent keeps serving, and the kernel's copy-on-write from [Virtual memory](/lessons/virtual-memory/) means they share memory pages until the parent modifies one. A crash loses everything since the last snapshot. **AOF**: every write command is appended to a log, and `appendfsync` (`always`, `everysec`, or `no`) decides how often it's `fsync`ed, the same durability-for-speed trade from [What happens when you save a file?](/lessons/files-and-disks/). For a pure cache, you can turn persistence off: losing a cache costs time, not data. That's the property to protect. **A cache must be safe to lose.** If deleting your Redis loses something that exists nowhere else, it's a database, and should be treated as one.

A note on the name: in 2024 Redis changed to a more restrictive license, and the Linux Foundation forked the last open-source version as Valkey, which major clouds now offer; Redis later added an AGPL option. For your purposes they speak the same protocol and the same commands. Check the current state if it matters for work.

### Caching LLM responses

Now the case you actually care about. Two very different caches go by the name "prompt caching", so first, a separation.

The [prefix cache](/lessons/prefix-caching/) reuses the **KV cache** for the shared beginning of prompts, inside the GPU. The model still runs and still generates every output token; it just skips recomputing attention for tokens it has seen. It's exact by construction and works for partially matching prompts.

A **response cache** sits in front of the server and skips the model entirely. It returns the stored text of a previous answer. That's thousands of times cheaper on a hit, and it only works when the whole request matches. The question that decides whether it's correct is the one from the top of the lesson: **what is the key?**

**Exact-match caching.** The key is a hash of everything that can change the output. Not the prompt. Everything:

- the model ID **and its revision** (weights hash, checkpoint path, or a version you bump),
- the chat template or prompt-template version, and any system prompt your server adds,
- all of `messages`, in order, and any tools or response format,
- every sampling parameter: `temperature`, `top_p`, `top_k`, `max_tokens`, `stop`, `seed`, penalties.

Turn that into a canonical string (JSON with sorted keys and no whitespace, so the same request always gives the same bytes) and hash it with SHA-256: `llm:v1:` plus 64 hex characters. On this Mac, hashing a request with a 2,000-character prompt took about 5 µs. Free.

Why so paranoid? Every field you leave out is a bug from the Problem. Leave out `max_tokens`, and a request for 16 tokens gets served a stored 512-token answer (or worse, the reverse: a truncated answer for someone who wanted the whole thing). Leave out the model revision, and you evaluate the old model forever.

Your cache key includes temperature. A user sends the same prompt five times at temperature 0.8, hoping for five different brainstorm ideas. What do they get from an exact-match cache? Is that a bug?

<Fragment slot="answer">

They get the same answer five times: the first sample, stored and replayed. Including temperature in the key keeps them from getting a temperature-0 answer, but it can't give them fresh randomness, because the cache's whole job is to return the stored result.

Whether that's a bug depends on what the request means. At temperature 0, the request asks for "the most likely answer", and replaying it is correct (it's even *more* consistent than the GPU, where greedy decoding can differ slightly between runs because batching changes the order of floating-point additions). At temperature 0.8, the request asks for "a sample from the distribution". A cache turns a distribution into a single point. Five calls are no longer five independent samples, and anything that relies on that (best-of-n, self-consistency voting, pass@k evals, brainstorming) silently breaks.

The usual policies: only cache when temperature is 0 (or a `seed` is set, so the request is really asking for one specific sample, and put the seed in the key); or let callers opt out with a header; or store several samples per key and hand them out in turn. Pick one on purpose.

</Fragment>

</Predict>

A few more rules for the response cache:

- **Only store complete, successful answers.** Not errors, not timeouts, not a stream that broke because the client hung up halfway. Check `finish_reason`.
- **Streaming still works.** On a hit, replay the stored text as SSE events, so clients don't need to know. Time to first token on a hit becomes a millisecond, which will look wonderful on your [scoreboard](/lessons/baseline-vs-fireworks/) and mean nothing about the model. Label cached runs separately.
- **Mind privacy.** If one user's conversation contains their own data, a shared cache keyed only on the text can only return that answer to someone who sent exactly the same text, which is fine. The danger comes with the next idea.

```python
# cache/lru.py
import time
from typing import Any, Callable, Hashable

class _Node:
    __slots__ = ("key", "value", "expires_at", "prev", "next")
    ...

class LRUCache:
    def __init__(self, capacity: int, default_ttl: float | None = None,
                 clock: Callable[[], float] = time.monotonic):
        """capacity >= 1. default_ttl in seconds, None = never expires.
        Set up the map and two sentinel nodes (head, tail) linked to each other.
        Counters: hits, misses, evictions, expirations."""
        ...

    def _unlink(self, node: _Node) -> None: ...
    def _push_front(self, node: _Node) -> None: ...

    def get(self, key: Hashable, default: Any = None) -> Any:
        """Miss -> default. Expired -> remove, count, default. Hit -> move to front."""
        ...

    def set(self, key: Hashable, value: Any, ttl: float | None = None) -> None:
        """Insert or overwrite at the front. ttl overrides default_ttl.
        Over capacity -> evict the node just before tail."""
        ...

    def __len__(self) -> int: ...
```

```python
# cache/test_lru.py
from lru import LRUCache

class FakeClock:
    def __init__(self): self.t = 0.0
    def __call__(self): return self.t

def test_evicts_least_recently_used():
    c = LRUCache(2)
    c.set("a", 1); c.set("b", 2)
    assert c.get("a") == 1          # a is now most recent
    c.set("c", 3)                   # so b is evicted, not a
    assert c.get("b") is None and c.get("a") == 1 and c.get("c") == 3

def test_ttl_expires():
    clk = FakeClock()
    c = LRUCache(10, default_ttl=5, clock=clk)
    c.set("a", 1)
    clk.t = 4.9; assert c.get("a") == 1
    clk.t = 5.0; assert c.get("a") is None
    assert len(c) == 0              # expired entries are actually removed
```

Hints: with sentinels, `_unlink` is two lines and `_push_front` is four. The most common bug is forgetting to delete from the map when you evict or expire, which the randomized test finds within a few hundred operations. Run with `pytest -q`.

## Break It

**1. Drop a field from the key.** Remove `max_tokens` from `cache_key`. Send a prompt with `max_tokens: 8`, then the same prompt with `max_tokens: 256`. The second user gets the 8-token answer, from cache, in a millisecond. Now make `cache_key` ignore `model_revision`, run a few eval prompts, then restart the server with a different model (any other small instruct model will do) and run them again. Every answer comes back from the old model, and the scores don't move. This is the most common cache bug in ML infrastructure, and the fix is the version-in-the-key discipline, not a better TTL.

**2. Make every key expire at once.** Warm the cache with 200 prompts in a burst with a fixed TTL of 60 seconds and no jitter, then send steady traffic over those prompts. At the 60-second mark the hit rate falls off a cliff and the upstream sees a wall of misses. Add ±10% jitter and rerun: the misses spread out over 12 seconds instead of arriving together.

**3. Fill Redis with `noeviction`.** Restart Redis with `--maxmemory 2mb --maxmemory-policy noeviction` and run the workload. Writes start failing with an error like `OOM command not allowed when used memory > 'maxmemory'`. Does your proxy still serve the request (it should: a failed cache write is not a failed request), or does it return 500? Then switch to `allkeys-lru` and watch `evicted_keys` climb in `INFO stats` instead.

**4. Scan through a small LRU.** Run a cyclic workload: prompts 1, 2, ..., 60, 1, 2, ..., 60, with a local LRU capacity of 50. The hit rate is zero. Not low: zero. Each prompt is evicted just before it comes back, because the 50 most recently used are always the 50 you won't need next. Increase capacity to 60 and it jumps to nearly 100%. It's the same failure the prefix cache lesson found with round-robin system prompts, and it's why databases and LFU/ARC-style caches treat one-time scans specially.

<summary>1. Why do caches work at all, and when don't they?</summary>

Because requests repeat, and unevenly. Recently used things tend to be used again soon (temporal locality), and popularity is lopsided (Zipf): a few items get a large share of all requests, so a cache holding a small fraction of items can answer a large fraction of requests. They don't work when requests rarely repeat (unique prompts, uniform popularity: hit rate is just cache size over the number of items), when a hit isn't much cheaper than a miss, or when the data changes faster than you can tolerate stale copies. First check how often your requests actually repeat.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Your cache has a 90% hit rate. Your boss wants it faster. Is 95% a small improvement?</summary>

No, it's about 2x. With hits nearly free and misses expensive, the average latency is about miss rate times miss cost. Going from 10% misses to 5% halves it. Going to 1% cuts it 10x. Think in miss rate. And separately: p50 is already at hit speed with 90% hits, and p99 is at miss speed until the miss rate goes below 1%. So if the complaint is about the tail, the hit rate has to go to 99%+, or the misses themselves have to get faster.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. How would you cache LLM responses, and what has to be in the key?</summary>

Cache-aside in front of the model server: hash a canonical serialization of the whole request, check Redis, on a miss call the model and store the complete answer with a long, jittered TTL. The key contains everything that changes the output: model and weights revision, prompt template version, the full messages and tools, and every sampling parameter (temperature, top_p, max_tokens, stop, seed). Only cache temperature-0 or seeded requests, because caching a sampled answer turns a distribution into one fixed sample. Only store complete successful responses. Make it fail open if Redis is down. Use coalescing so a popular prompt isn't computed many times at once. And keep it separate from prefix caching, which reuses KV inside the GPU and still runs the model; the response cache skips the model entirely but needs an exact match.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. What is a cache stampede and how do you prevent it?</summary>

A hot key expires and every request that arrives before it's recomputed misses too, so the backend gets many identical expensive requests at once. On an inference server behind one GPU, that means hundreds of queued copies of the same generation. Fixes: request coalescing (singleflight), so only the first miss computes and others wait on its result; across processes, a short-lived lock in Redis with SET NX PX; jittered TTLs so keys don't expire together; stale-while-revalidate, which keeps serving the old value while one background refresh runs; or probabilistic early refresh before the TTL.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. You go from one inference server to 50 replicas and 10,000 requests a second. How does your cache design change?</summary>

A per-process cache now has 50 separate copies with 1/50th the reuse each, so you want a shared tier: Redis (or Redis Cluster, which splits keys across nodes using 16,384 hash slots, so capacity and throughput grow with nodes). Then do the numbers. 10,000 requests a second is fine for a small Redis cluster, since one node does on the order of 100k simple operations a second. Memory: if responses average 2 KB and you want to keep 10 million of them, that's 20 GB plus overhead, a few nodes. Watch for hot keys: one popular prompt maps to one node, and a single key can saturate it; the fix is a small in-process cache on each replica in front of Redis (a two-tier cache) with a short TTL. Coalescing must work across machines (a lock in Redis), or one expiring hot key sends 50 copies to the GPUs. Make the whole thing fail open with tight timeouts, since a Redis outage must degrade to slower, not down. And route similar prompts to the same replicas anyway, for the prefix cache inside the GPU.

</details>

## Go Deeper

- **Rajesh Nishtala et al., "Scaling Memcache at Facebook"**, NSDI 2013. The best real-world account of every problem in this lesson at enormous scale: invalidation across regions, leases to stop stale sets and stampedes, cold clusters, hot keys.
- **Redis docs: "Key eviction" and "Redis persistence"** (on redis.io). How `maxmemory-policy`, the sampled LRU and LFU actually work, and the honest trade-offs between RDB and AOF. Short and precise.
- **Nimrod Megiddo and Dharmendra S. Modha, "ARC: A Self-Tuning, Low Overhead Replacement Cache"**, FAST 2003. A beautiful paper: how to get the benefits of both LRU and LFU, and adapt between them, in constant time per request.
- **Lee Breslau, Pei Cao, Li Fan, Graham Phillips, Scott Shenker, "Web Caching and Zipf-like Distributions: Evidence and Implications"**, INFOCOM 1999. The measurement that showed web traffic follows Zipf and derived what that means for hit rate vs cache size.
- **Andrea Vattani, Flavio Chierichetti, Keegan Lowenstein, "Optimal Probabilistic Cache Stampede Prevention"**, VLDB 2015. The XFetch algorithm: refresh early with a probability that makes the stampede vanish, with a one-line formula.
- **Mark Nottingham, [RFC 5861: HTTP Cache-Control Extensions for Stale Content](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5861)**, and **[RFC 9111: HTTP Caching](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111)**. Where `stale-while-revalidate` comes from, and the standard every browser and CDN cache follows.
- **Go's [singleflight package](https://pkg.go.dev/golang.org/x/sync/singleflight)** and Brad Fitzpatrick's **groupcache**. About a hundred lines that are the canonical implementation of request coalescing. Read the source.
- **GPTCache** (zilliztech/GPTCache on GitHub). A popular open-source semantic cache for LLMs. Read how it configures similarity thresholds and evaluation, with your Task 5 results in mind.
