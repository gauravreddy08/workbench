Batching means letting the GPU work on many people's answers in the same pass instead of one person's at a time. Continuous batching goes one step further: people can join and leave that shared pass at any moment, like passengers on a bus that never waits at the terminal.

This is how inference companies make money. A GPU costs the same per hour whether it writes 17 word-pieces a second or 700, and batching is most of the difference between the two. It is also the row on your scoreboard that hurt most: at 16 users, your naive server made the last person wait about five minutes for their first word. And when an interviewer asks how Fireworks serves thousands of strangers on a handful of GPUs, this is the answer.

It builds on a lot of what you have done. [Why ~17 tokens per second?](/lessons/bandwidth-bound/) showed that each step is mostly hauling the model out of memory, and that haul can be shared. [The baseline](/lessons/baseline-vs-fireworks/) gave you the server, the benchmark and the scoreboard you are about to upgrade. ## The Problem

Open the results from the baseline lesson and look at c = 16. Sixteen workers, each asking for 256 tokens. Your naive server served them one at a time, so the sixteenth person waited about five minutes for their first word, and the server's total throughput was the same ~13 tok/s it managed for one person. You added fifteen users and got only waiting.

Now look at the roofline from the bandwidth lesson. One decode step reads 16 GB of weights to do 16 GFLOPs of math. The GPU spends more than 99% of every step waiting for bytes. And yet the server makes each user wait for every other user's entire answer, while the math units do nothing.

That is the absurd part. The GPU has room for dozens of users in every step. The server only lets one in.

Fixing it sounds like one line: "process several requests together". It isn't. Requests arrive at random times. Prompts have different lengths. One user wants a one-word answer and the next wants an essay. Put them in a batch naively and the one-word answer waits for the essay, the essay waits for a batch to fill up, and the user who arrives one millisecond after the batch started waits for all of it. Getting batching right for LLMs took the field until 2022, and the fix changed what an inference server is.

<Origin year="2022" title="Orca: schedule iterations, not requests">

Batching in model serving is old. Clipper (Crankshaw et al., NSDI 2017), TensorFlow Serving and NVIDIA's Triton all batched requests: gather a few, run them through the model together, return all the answers. That works beautifully for an image classifier, where every request is one forward pass of the same shape and takes the same time.

LLMs broke the assumption. A request is not one forward pass; it is hundreds, one per output token, and nobody knows in advance how many. Serving systems in 2021 (NVIDIA's FasterTransformer, served through Triton) still batched at the level of whole requests: a batch went in, ran until its longest member was done, and came out together.

In 2022 Gyeong-In Yu, Joo Seong Jeong, Geon-Woo Kim, Soojeong Kim and Byung-Gon Chun at Seoul National University and FriendliAI published Orca at OSDI. Their idea: let the scheduler make a decision after *every iteration*, every single forward pass. A finished sequence leaves immediately; a new one joins at the next step. They called it iteration-level scheduling, reported up to 36.9× the throughput of FasterTransformer at the same latency on a GPT-3 175B model, and within about a year every serious engine (vLLM, Hugging Face TGI, TensorRT-LLM, which calls it "in-flight batching") worked this way. The name that stuck is continuous batching.

</Origin>

## The Idea

### One read, many users

Recap the kitchen from the bandwidth lesson: a chef who chops a thousand onions a second, fed by one slow delivery van. Decode is the van's speed. Every step, the full model drives across town so the chef can do one token of work.

Batching is noticing that the van can carry everyone's onions. Put 64 users' latest tokens in the same step and each weight, once it arrives, is used 64 times instead of once. The expensive part of the step, reading 16 GB, happens once. What grows with the batch is only what is *not* shared:

- **Math.** 64 tokens of matrix-vector work instead of one. Still tiny next to the ridge point.
- **The KV caches.** Each user's past keys and values are their own bytes, and attention reads all of them every step. Weights are shared across a batch; <Term w="KV caches">The stored keys and values of every past token of one sequence, in every layer. Attention reads the whole thing at every decode step. 128 KiB per token for Llama 3.1 8B in bf16.</Term> are not.

So a decode step costs roughly:

```text
step time  ≈  (weight bytes + B × KV bytes per user) ÷ bandwidth

Llama 3.1 8B, bf16, 1K tokens of context per user, spec-sheet 273 GB/s:
  weights      16.06 GB            → 58.8 ms, paid once per step
  KV per user  1,024 × 128 KiB     → 0.134 GB → 0.49 ms each
```

<Fragment slot="answer">

About 1.5× longer: 58.8 + 64 × 0.49 ≈ 90 ms instead of about 59 ms. But that step now produces 64 tokens instead of one, so total throughput goes from about 17 tok/s to about 64 / 0.090 ≈ 710 tok/s, roughly 42× more, while each user's own stream slows from 17 to 11 tok/s. Math never even enters into it at this size: 64 tokens × 16 GFLOPs is about 1 TFLOP per step, under 10 ms at 125 TFLOP/s, and it overlaps with the memory traffic anyway.

</Fragment>

Read the table top to bottom. Up to a batch of 16 the step barely moves; throughput is close to free. By 64 the KV caches are a third of the traffic and each user feels it. By 256 they are most of the traffic, each user gets 5 tok/s, and you are trading their experience for your throughput. Notice what never showed up: the compute roof. At 1K context the Spark runs out of *bandwidth for KV caches* long before it runs out of math. (Real servers land below these ceilings, as your naive one did. The shape is what matters.)

That is the whole economic case. One GPU-hour costs the same whether it makes 17 tokens a second or 700. Batching is how an inference company divides that hour by 40.

### Static batching and its three taxes

The simplest way to batch: collect B requests, pad them to the same shape, run them through `generate` together, return everyone's answer when it finishes. This is <Term w="static batching">Forming a fixed group of requests and running the whole group from start to finish together. Nobody joins or leaves until the whole group is done.</Term>, and it was the standard way to batch models before Orca. It has three problems, and each one has a cost you can see.

**Tax 1: <Term w="padding">Filler tokens added to the shorter sequences so a batch is one rectangular tensor. They are masked out of attention so they do not change the answer, but the GPU still computes and stores them.</Term>.** A GPU wants a rectangle: B rows of the same length. Prompts are not the same length, so the short ones get filler tokens, plus an <Term w="attention mask">A tensor of 1s and 0s, one per token position, telling attention which positions are real. Positions marked 0 get a score of minus infinity, so they receive zero weight after softmax.</Term> so attention ignores them. The answer comes out right, but the filler is real computation and real KV cache memory. For decoder models you pad on the *left*, so every row's last real token sits in the same final column, which is where the next token gets appended.

**Tax 2: waiting for the longest.** Output lengths vary wildly: "yes" is one token, a code review is a thousand. A static batch runs until its longest member is done. Every row that finished early keeps its place in every step until then, producing padding tokens that get thrown away. In the figure, half of all slot-steps are wasted. In a real chat workload, where one long answer in a batch is common, it can be worse.

**Tax 3: <Term w="head-of-line blocking">When one slow item at the front of a line holds up everything behind it, even items that would be quick. The term comes from networking: a packet at the head of a queue blocking the ones behind.</Term>.** R5 arrives a moment after the batch started. The GPU had room for it in every step after that (R4 left early), but the batch is closed. R5 waits for the whole thing, then gets its turn. Its time to first token is the length of the *longest answer in someone else's batch*.

### Don't wait forever: dynamic batching

There is a fourth problem hiding in "collect B requests". What if only three show up? A pure static batcher waits for the fourth. At 3 a.m., when one person sends one request, they wait until three strangers wake up.

<Predict id="static-deadlock">

Your static batching server uses B = 16 and waits for 16 requests before running anything. Your benchmark harness runs a closed loop at c = 4, warming up with 3 requests first. What does the benchmark report?

<Fragment slot="answer">

Nothing, ever. In a closed loop there are never more than 4 requests in the system (and during the sequential warm-up, only one). The batch never reaches 16, so the server never runs anything, and the workers wait forever on their first request. It is a deadlock, and it also bites at c = 16: when the run winds down and fewer than 16 requests are left, the last batch never fills, and the run never finishes. I watched exactly this happen while testing this lesson's code: the final few requests of a run just hung.

</Fragment>

</Predict>

The fix is a deadline. <Term w="dynamic batching">Static batching with a time limit: start the batch when it is full or when the oldest waiting request has waited a maximum time, whichever comes first. Triton's version of this is called the dynamic batcher.</Term> starts a batch when it is full *or* when the oldest waiting request has waited for a fixed <Term w="max wait window">The longest a request may sit in the queue waiting for a batch to fill. Every request pays up to this much extra latency, in exchange for a fuller batch. Typical values are a few to a few tens of milliseconds.</Term>, whichever comes first. NVIDIA Triton's dynamic batcher calls this `max_queue_delay_microseconds`. The window is a dial: zero means "never wait, batch whatever is there", large means "wait for fuller batches". Each request pays up to one window of extra latency, and in return the GPU runs fuller steps.

Dynamic batching fixes the deadlock. It does not fix taxes 2 and 3. Once a batch starts, it is still closed until its longest member finishes.

### Continuous batching: decide at every step

Go back to what the GPU actually does. A request is not one big operation. It is a prefill followed by hundreds of tiny, identical decode steps, and each step is a natural break point. At every step the scheduler could ask: who is done? Who is waiting? Who should be in the next step?

That is <Term w="iteration-level scheduling">Making the scheduling decision before every forward pass ("iteration") instead of once per request. Sequences join the running batch the step after they arrive and leave the step they finish.</Term>, Orca's idea, and today everyone calls it <Term w="continuous batching">Iteration-level scheduling applied to LLM serving: the batch is a changing set of sequences, each at its own position in its own answer. Also called in-flight batching (NVIDIA's term) or dynamic batching at the token level.</Term>. The batch stops being a group that travels together. It becomes a bus with seats: at every stop someone gets off and someone gets on, and the bus never waits at the terminal.

The server becomes a loop that never ends:

1. **Admit.** If there are free seats and requests waiting, take some in. Run their prefill (this produces their first token).
2. **Step.** Run one forward pass for every sequence in the batch. Each gets one new token.
3. **Emit.** Send each new token to its own user's stream.
4. **Evict.** Any sequence that just hit its stop token or its `max_tokens` leaves. Its seat and its KV cache are freed at once.

Things to try, in order:

- **Mixed lengths, trickle in, 4 slots.** No batching takes 160 steps for everything and uses a quarter of the GPU's slots. Static gets that to 92 steps, but its slot strip is full of grey: seats held by finished sequences. Continuous finishes in 46 steps with about 87% of slots busy, and its mean time to first token falls from 28 steps (static) to under 6.
- **Dynamic, drag max wait.** At 0 it launches tiny batches immediately; turn it up and batches get fuller but everyone waits longer to start. Neither end fixes the grey.
- **Equal lengths, all at once.** Static, dynamic and continuous become *identical*. When everyone arrives together and wants the same amount, there is nothing for continuous batching to fix. Remember this one; it matters for your benchmark.
- **8 slots, mixed lengths.** Continuous gets every request its first token within a step or so of arriving, because there is always a free seat. The cost of more slots is memory, which is the last section.

Your harness runs a <Term w="closed loop">A load test where each of c workers sends its next request only after the previous answer finishes, so exactly c requests are always in flight.</Term> with a fixed `max_tokens` and `ignore_eos`: every request has the *same* length, and at c = 16 all sixteen start at once and finish at once. That is the "equal lengths, all at once" case. So expect dynamic batching to look surprisingly good at c = 16 in your results, and plan one extra run with mixed lengths to see the real difference.

### Prefill and decode in the same step

There is a wrinkle the figure hides. A new request needs a prefill: its whole prompt, hundreds or thousands of tokens, through the model in one pass. Running sequences need one decode token each. What happens to them while a newcomer prefills?

In the simplest design, which you are about to build, a step is *either* a prefill for the newcomers *or* a decode for everyone else. When a 2,000-token prompt arrives, every running stream freezes for that prefill. From the bandwidth lesson, prefill of 2,000 tokens is about 2 × 8 billion × 2,000 = 32 trillion operations; on the Spark that is on the order of half a second or more. Sixty-three users see their text stop for half a second because one user pasted a document. Their <Term w="ITL">Inter-token latency: the gap between consecutive tokens of one stream. A prefill stall shows up as one huge gap (your harness's max_gap).</Term> has a spike in it.

Orca mixed them: one forward pass could contain the newcomer's prompt tokens *and* everyone else's decode tokens, because the big matrix multiplies do not care which request a token belongs to. Only attention does (each token must attend to its own sequence's past), so Orca ran attention separately per request and batched everything else, a trick it called <Term w="selective batching">Orca's technique: flatten all tokens from all requests in a step into one list for the matrix multiplies (which treat tokens independently), but split them back up by request for attention (which must not mix sequences).</Term>.

That fixes the separate step, but not the size. A 2,000-token prompt still makes one giant step. <Term w="chunked prefill">Splitting a long prompt's prefill into pieces of a few hundred tokens and doing one piece per step, alongside the running decodes, so no single step gets much slower than the others. From the SARATHI and Sarathi-Serve papers; now standard in vLLM and SGLang.</Term> (Agrawal et al., the SARATHI work in 2023 and Sarathi-Serve at OSDI 2024) splits the long prompt into chunks and does one chunk per step, filling a fixed per-step <Term w="token budget">The maximum number of tokens (prefill plus decode) the scheduler will put in one forward pass. It bounds how slow any one step can get.</Term> with decodes first and prefill chunks after. The decodes stay smooth; the newcomer's TTFT gets a little longer. And there is a nice bonus hiding in the roofline: decode steps are memory-bound and prefill is compute-bound, so mixing them puts both halves of the chip to work in the same pass.

### The trade: throughput vs latency

Batching is not free for the user. Every user in a batch shares the step, so a bigger batch is a slower step for everyone: in Figure 001, per-user speed falls from 17 to 11 tok/s as the batch grows from 1 to 64. Prefills from newcomers stall or slow the step further. And a dynamic batcher's wait window is pure added latency.

So a serving system does not maximize throughput. It maximizes throughput *subject to a promise*. That promise is an <Term w="SLO">Service level objective: a target for a metric that you commit to, such as "p99 time to first token under 1 second" or "at least 10 tokens per second per stream".</Term>. A typical LLM SLO has two parts: TTFT (how long until text starts, usually as a p99) and ITL (how steady the text is, usually p50 or p90). Reading speed is a useful anchor: people read roughly 4 to 6 words a second, so a stream much below 8 to 10 tok/s starts to feel like waiting.

The number that captures "throughput, but only the useful kind" is <Term w="goodput">Throughput counting only requests that met their SLO. A server doing 700 tok/s where half the requests blew their TTFT target has a goodput of roughly 350. The DistServe paper (OSDI 2024) popularized it for LLM serving.</Term>. Pushing the batch size up raises throughput until requests start missing their deadlines; past that knee, goodput falls even while raw throughput keeps climbing.

On the Spark with an 8B model, memory is generous: at your benchmark's ~300 tokens of context per request, a thousand users fit. Bandwidth (Figure 001) and your server's overhead will stop you long before memory does. That is unusual, and it is the Spark's 128 GB talking. Move the same model to a 24 GB gaming card and the picture flips: 16 GB of weights leaves maybe 6 GB for caches, about 46,000 tokens, so around 11 users at 4K context. On a data-center GPU serving a 70B model, memory is almost always the wall. That is why the next big idea in serving, vLLM's paged attention, is about memory, not speed.

Three things make the memory wall worse than the table:

- **Padding.** The simple cache you are about to build is one rectangular tensor: every row is as long as the longest row. A batch with one 4,000-token conversation and sixty-three short chats reserves 4,000 tokens for everyone.
- **You do not know the future.** A request that asks for `max_tokens` 4,096 *might* use all of it. Reserve for the worst case and you fit fewer users; reserve less and you may run out mid-answer.
- **Running out mid-answer.** When memory fills, real engines <Term w="preempt">Pause a running sequence to free its memory: either copy its KV cache to CPU memory (swap) or throw it away and recompute it later from the prompt and the tokens generated so far (recompute).</Term> a running sequence and bring it back later. Your server will simply refuse to admit more than a fixed number of sequences.

[Paged attention and prefix caching](/lessons/prefix-caching/) fixes the first two by storing caches in small fixed-size blocks, like the pages in [virtual memory](/lessons/virtual-memory/), so a sequence only holds what it has used.

<Task id="t2" title="Dynamic batching: a queue, a collector, one padded generate" agent="hands">

`server_static.py`. The shape of the whole thing:

- The endpoint is now `async def`. It wraps the request in a `Job` (the request plus its own `asyncio.Queue` of output tokens), puts the job on one shared `pending` queue, and streams whatever arrives on its own queue.
- One background task, the **collector**, takes jobs off `pending` and forms batches: up to `MAX_BATCH` jobs, or whatever has arrived when the oldest has waited `MAX_WAIT_MS`.
- The collector runs each batch with one `model.generate` call on left-padded inputs, in a worker thread, and waits for it to finish before forming the next batch. That waiting is static batching's head-of-line blocking, on purpose.
- A custom streamer gets the batch's new tokens after every step and routes each one to its job's queue.

The streamer is the interesting part. `generate` calls `streamer.put(...)` once with the prompt ids, then once per step with a tensor of shape `[B]`: one new token per row. It calls `streamer.end()` when it is done. You write the class:

```python
from transformers.generation.streamers import BaseStreamer

class BatchStreamer(BaseStreamer):
    def __init__(self, jobs: list["Job"], loop: asyncio.AbstractEventLoop): ...

    def put(self, value):
        """First call: the prompt ids. Skip it. Every later call: value[i] is row i's new token.
        For each job not yet done: count it, decide if this token finishes the job
        (its own max_tokens, or an EOS when not ignore_eos), and hand (token, done) to the job.
        You are on generate's thread, not the event loop's, so you may NOT call
        job.q.put_nowait directly. Use self.loop.call_soon_threadsafe(job.q.put_nowait, item)."""

    def end(self):
        """Mark any job that is still open as done, so no client waits forever."""
```

And the collector:

```python
pending: asyncio.Queue  # create it inside FastAPI's lifespan, on the running loop

async def collector():
    loop = asyncio.get_running_loop()
    while True:
        batch = [await pending.get()]          # sleep until there is at least one job
        # keep taking jobs until len(batch) == MAX_BATCH or the window since the first job closes.
        # asyncio.wait_for(pending.get(), timeout) raises TimeoutError when the window runs out.
        # MAX_WAIT_MS = inf means pure static batching: wait for a full batch, forever.
        await asyncio.to_thread(run_batch, batch, loop)
```

Hints:

- **Left padding.** `tokenizer.padding_side = "left"`, and set `tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token` if the tokenizer has none (Llama 3's does not). Build each prompt string with `apply_chat_template(..., tokenize=False)`, then tokenize the list with `padding=True, add_special_tokens=False` (the template already added the begin-of-text token).
- **One `max_new_tokens` per batch.** It is the batch's largest `max_tokens`. Each job stops *emitting* at its own limit, but `generate` keeps computing its row until the longest is done. That is tax 2, and you can see it in your results.
- **`ignore_eos`.** If every job in the batch has it, pass `min_new_tokens=max_new_tokens` like the naive server did.
- **Usage.** Record each job's prompt length from the attention mask (`mask.sum()`), and count completion tokens in the endpoint as they arrive.
- **The endpoint reads with `await job.q.get()`**, feeds token ids to a `Detok`, and yields SSE events exactly like the naive server, ending with `usage` and `[DONE]`.
- **Start the collector in a lifespan handler** (`@asynccontextmanager async def lifespan(app)`, then `FastAPI(lifespan=lifespan)`), so it runs on the same event loop as the endpoints.
- If `run_batch` raises, `asyncio.to_thread` re-raises inside the collector, the collector task dies, and every future request hangs. Wrap it in `try/except`, log, and mark the batch's jobs done.

<Task id="t3" title="The continuous batching engine" agent="hands">

This is the heart of the lesson: your own decode loop over a changing set of sequences. Correctness first; speed comes from engines you will read in [serving engines](/lessons/serving-engines/).

**The design.** The running batch keeps one padded KV cache: per layer, keys and values of shape `[B, kv_heads, L, head_dim]`, where row *i* is sequence *i*, left-padded so that every row's latest token sits in column L − 1. Next to it, an attention mask `[B, L]` with 1 for real positions. Each decode step appends one column. Admitting sequences means prefilling them, then left-padding the two caches to the same length and stacking them. Evicting means keeping only some rows, then trimming leading columns no remaining row uses.

The cache plumbing depends on the transformers version, so take these helpers as given. They convert between Hugging Face's <Term w="DynamicCache">Hugging Face's default KV cache object: per layer, a keys tensor and a values tensor that grow by one position each step. The forward pass takes it as past_key_values and returns it grown.</Term> and plain per-layer tensors:

```python
# engine.py
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
import torch
from transformers import DynamicCache

# Version-specific: checked on transformers 5.x. On 4.4x the equivalents were
# DynamicCache.from_legacy_cache(tuple_of_(k, v)) and cache.key_cache[i] / cache.value_cache[i].
def kv_tensors(cache) -> list[tuple[torch.Tensor, torch.Tensor]]:
    return [(layer.keys, layer.values) for layer in cache.layers]

def make_cache(kvs) -> DynamicCache:
    return DynamicCache(ddp_cache_data=kvs)

def left_pad(x: torch.Tensor, n: int, dim: int) -> torch.Tensor:
    """Prepend n zeros along dim."""
    if n == 0:
        return x
    shape = list(x.shape); shape[dim] = n
    return torch.cat([x.new_zeros(shape), x], dim=dim)
```

The engine is yours:

```python
@dataclass
class Seq:
    rid: str
    prompt: list[int]
    max_new: int
    ignore_eos: bool = False
    out: list[int] = field(default_factory=list)
    done: bool = False


class Engine:
    def __init__(self, model, eos: set[int], max_batch: int = 64):
        self.model, self.eos, self.max_batch = model, eos, max_batch
        self.waiting: list[Seq] = []
        self.running: list[Seq] = []
        self.cache = None   # DynamicCache for self.running, or None when empty
        self.mask = None    # [B, L] long tensor

    def pick(self, seqs, logits) -> list[int]:
        """Greedy next token per row. For ignore_eos rows, set the EOS logits to -inf first."""

    def prefill(self, seqs) -> tuple[DynamicCache, torch.Tensor]:
        """Left-pad the prompts into one [n, P] batch, run one forward pass,
        append each sequence's first token, return (cache, mask)."""

    def merge(self, cache, mask):
        """Add newly prefilled rows to the running cache: left-pad both to the same L, then cat on dim 0."""

    def evict(self, keep: list[int]):
        """Keep only rows `keep` of cache and mask, then drop leading columns no remaining row uses."""

    def step(self) -> list[Seq]:
        """One iteration. Admit up to the free seats; if anyone was admitted, this step is their prefill.
        Otherwise decode one token for every running sequence. Then mark and evict finished ones.
        Return the sequences that got a new token this step (so the server can stream them)."""
```

Hints, roughly in the order you will need them:

- **Position ids.** With left padding, a row's padding must not count as position. In prefill, `position_ids = (mask.cumsum(-1) - 1).clamp(min=0)`. In decode, the new token's position is the number of real tokens already in that row: `mask.sum(-1, keepdim=True)`, computed *before* you append the new mask column.
- **The decode call.** `model(input_ids=[B, 1] last tokens, attention_mask=mask with the new column, position_ids=..., past_key_values=self.cache, use_cache=True)`. The returned `past_key_values` is the grown cache. `out.logits[:, -1]` is the next-token scores.
- **Only the last logits.** In prefill, a 2,000-token prompt × 128,256 vocabulary entries is half a gigabyte of logits you do not need. Recent transformers accept `logits_to_keep=1` in the forward call; check your version.
- **Finished.** A sequence is done when `len(out) == max_new`, or its last token is in `eos` and it is not `ignore_eos`. Its first token comes from prefill, so a `max_new` of 1 finishes in the admit step.
- **Prefill and decode in separate steps.** This is the simple design from the Idea section: a step that admits newcomers does only their prefill, and running sequences wait one step. You will see the cost in Break It.
- **Seats, not memory.** `max_batch` is your only admission limit. A memory-aware version would also check `sum of (len(prompt) + max_new)` against a KV budget; add it if you have time.
- **Copies.** Every `merge` and `evict` copies the whole cache. Happens a few times per finished request, not every step, so it is fine for now. (HF's `DynamicCache` also grows each layer with `torch.cat` every step, which copies too. This is one of the things paged attention exists to stop.)

</Task>

The engine is the kind of code that runs, produces fluent text, and is subtly wrong (a position off by one, a mask column missing). Fluent text is not evidence. This test is: feed requests in at different steps, with different prompt and output lengths, and require every sequence to match plain one-at-a-time greedy `generate` exactly.

```python
# test_engine.py: run on your Mac with the tiny model in fp32.
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer
from engine import Engine, Seq

M = "HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-135M-Instruct"
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(M)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(M, dtype=torch.float32).eval()
eos = model.generation_config.eos_token_id
eos = set(eos if isinstance(eos, list) else [eos])

prompts = ["Write a haiku about memory bandwidth.", "What is 17 times 23?",
           "Name three rivers in Europe and one fact about each.", "Explain batching to a child.",
           "hi", "Tell me a long story about a dragon who learns to code and opens a bakery."]
lens = [12, 30, 5, 40, 30, 12]
ids = [tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": p}], add_generation_prompt=True,
                               tokenize=True, return_dict=True)["input_ids"] for p in prompts]

ref = []
for p, n in zip(ids, lens):
    o = model.generate(torch.tensor([p]), max_new_tokens=n, min_new_tokens=n, do_sample=False)
    ref.append(o[0, len(p):].tolist())

eng = Engine(model, eos, max_batch=3)        # 3 seats for 6 requests: forces queueing and reuse
arrive = {0: [0, 1], 3: [2], 4: [3, 4], 20: [5]}
done, t = {}, 0
while len(done) < len(prompts):
    for i in arrive.get(t, []):
        eng.waiting.append(Seq(str(i), ids[i], lens[i], ignore_eos=True))
    for s in eng.step():
        if s.done:
            done[s.rid] = s.out
    t += 1

for i in range(len(prompts)):
    assert done[str(i)] == ref[i], (i, tok.decode(ref[i]), tok.decode(done[str(i)]))
print(f"all {len(prompts)} match after {t} steps")
```

If your `step` reports finished sequences differently, adapt the collection loop. `ignore_eos=True` matters: `min_new_tokens` in the reference suppresses EOS, so your engine must too, or the two diverge at the first end-of-turn token. (That is exactly the mismatch this test caught while the lesson was being written.)

Two things this test will not catch, so know them. In **bf16 on the GPU**, batched and unbatched runs can legitimately drift apart after a while: padding and batch size change the shapes of the matrix multiplies, which changes the order of floating-point additions, and a near-tie between two tokens can flip. Test exactness in fp32; in bf16, check the first few dozen tokens match and the rest are sensible. And it does not test streaming or threading; the harness does that.

The endpoint tokenizes the prompt with the chat template, builds a `Seq`, attaches `seq.q = asyncio.Queue()` and `seq.loop = asyncio.get_running_loop()`, puts it on `inbox`, and streams. Send the token *count* through the queue, not the token, and decode `seq.out[:n]` on the event loop side: the engine thread keeps appending to `seq.out`, and a count is a consistent snapshot.

Why a thread and not an asyncio task? `eng.step()` blocks for a whole forward pass. On the event loop it would freeze every connection for 60-100 ms per step, forever (the lesson from [Waiting vs working](/lessons/waiting-vs-working/)). A thread lets the GPU work while the event loop keeps streaming; PyTorch releases the GIL inside its kernels, so the two really do overlap ([Processes, threads and the GIL](/lessons/processes-threads-gil/)).

When it works, have an agent review it against this list: no blocking call on the event loop; no asyncio queue touched from the engine thread except through `call_soon_threadsafe`; an exception inside `step()` is logged and fails the affected requests instead of killing the thread silently; a client that disconnects has its sequence marked done so it stops using a seat (the bug you noted at the end of the baseline lesson).

## Break It

**Pure static, low load.** Start `server_static.py` with `MAX_WAIT_MS=inf` and `MAX_BATCH=16`, then run the harness at c = 4. It never returns. Then try c = 16: it gets most of the way and hangs on the last few requests, because the final batch never fills. (This happened, exactly so, while this lesson's code was being tested.) Lesson: every batcher needs a deadline, and a closed-loop benchmark is very good at finding the missing one.

**Pad on the right.** Set `tokenizer.padding_side = "right"` in the static server and send two requests of very different prompt lengths at once. The short one's answer turns to nonsense or runs on oddly, because its new tokens are being appended after a run of pad tokens instead of after its prompt. Transformers prints a warning about right-padding for decoder-only models; you will now never ignore it. Lesson: for decoder models the newest token must be in the last column of every row.

**Break the position ids.** In the engine's decode step, use the cache's column count (`mask.shape[1]`) as every row's position instead of each row's own count of real tokens. Run `test_engine.py`. When this was tried on the tiny model, two of the six sequences still matched and four drifted: rows that carried little or no padding were barely affected, padded rows were off. Drifted text can still read fine, which is why you need the test. Lesson: padding has to be invisible to *every* part of the model, and <Term w="RoPE">Rotary position embedding: Llama encodes each token's position by rotating its query and key vectors by an angle that depends on the position id. Wrong position ids mean wrong rotations.</Term> positions are part of the model.

**Paste a novel.** With the continuous server running a steady c = 32 of short requests, send one request with a 4,000-token prompt (`prompts/long.jsonl` doubled). Look at `max_gap` for the other streams during that moment: every one of them stalls for the full prefill, probably most of a second. Then think about how you would split that prefill into 512-token chunks: each chunk is a forward pass over 512 new tokens attending to the cache built so far, which your `prefill` can almost do already if it accepts a starting cache. Lesson: the scheduler's job is not just who is in the batch, but how much work goes into each step.

## Explain It Back

Answer out loud, in plain words, before opening the answer.

<details>
<summary>1. Why does serving 64 users at once make each token cheaper, and why doesn't it make each user slower by 64×?</summary>

A decode step is dominated by reading the weights from memory: 16 GB for an 8B model in bf16, about 59 ms on the Spark. That read is the same whether one user's token is in the step or 64 users' tokens are, because each weight, once it arrives, can be multiplied against all 64 token vectors. So the step gets only a bit longer (each user's KV cache is extra bytes, and there is a little more math), while it produces 64 tokens instead of one. The GPU-hour costs the same and makes about 40× more tokens, so each token costs about a fortieth. Each user's own speed drops only by the step-time increase, about 17 to 11 tok/s at 1K context.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What's wrong with static batching for LLMs, and how does continuous batching fix it?</summary>

Static batching runs a fixed group from start to end together. Three costs: short prompts get padded to the longest, which is wasted compute and memory; short answers keep their slot until the longest answer in the batch finishes; and a request that arrives just after a batch starts waits for the whole batch. Plus, without a time limit it can wait forever for a batch to fill. Dynamic batching adds a max wait window, which fixes the waiting-to-fill problem but not the others. Continuous batching makes the scheduling decision at every decode step instead of once per batch: a finished sequence leaves immediately, a new one joins at the next step, so seats are never held empty and nobody waits for someone else's long answer.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. How does this scale: how many users can your Spark serve with an 8B model at 10 tok/s per stream?</summary>

10 tok/s per stream means a decode step must take at most 100 ms. Step time is about (16.06 GB + B × KV bytes per user) ÷ 273 GB/s. 100 ms × 273 GB/s = 27.3 GB per step, minus 16.06 GB of weights leaves 11.2 GB for KV caches. At 1K tokens of context each (0.134 GB), that is about 83 users; at 512 tokens, about 167. Those are spec-sheet ceilings. My server will hit fewer because of Python overhead and prefill steps, so I would measure it: run the harness at increasing c and find the highest c where per-stream tok/s p50 stays above 10 and TTFT p99 stays within its SLO. Memory is not the limit here (90 GB of KV pool holds hundreds of thousands of tokens); bandwidth is. On a 24 GB GPU it would be memory: only about 6 GB left for caches.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A user pastes a 4,000-token document into a busy continuous-batching server. What happens to everyone else, and what is chunked prefill?</summary>

The new prompt needs a prefill: all 4,000 tokens through the model, which is compute-bound and takes far longer than a decode step. If the server does that prefill as its own step, every other running stream gets no token during it, so their text visibly pauses. Even if prefill and decode are mixed in one forward pass, that pass is huge, so it is still a long step. Chunked prefill splits the prompt into pieces of a few hundred tokens and adds one piece to each step, next to everyone's single decode token, up to a fixed token budget. Every step stays about the same length, the other streams keep moving, and the newcomer's first token arrives a bit later. It also uses the idle compute in memory-bound decode steps for the prefill work.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Throughput keeps rising as you add users. Why would you ever cap the batch size?</summary>

Because users do not experience throughput; they experience time to first token and tokens per second. Bigger batches make each step slower, so every stream gets slower, and more newcomers means more prefill stalls. Past some point most requests miss their SLO. The right target is goodput: throughput counting only requests that met their latency targets. You cap the batch where goodput peaks, which is usually well below the batch size that maximizes raw throughput. Memory gives a second, hard cap: every sequence's KV cache must fit, and running out mid-answer means preempting someone.

</details>

- **Gyeong-In Yu, Joo Seong Jeong, Geon-Woo Kim, Soojeong Kim, Byung-Gon Chun, "Orca: A Distributed Serving System for Transformer-Based Generative Models", OSDI 2022.** The paper that introduced iteration-level scheduling and selective batching. Sections 2 and 3 are the clearest statement of why request-level batching fails for generation.
- **Cade Daniel et al., "How continuous batching enables 23x throughput in LLM inference while reducing p50 latency", Anyscale blog, 2023.** The friendly version, with diagrams of exactly the static-vs-continuous picture in Figure 003 and benchmarks across real engines.
- **Woosuk Kwon et al., "Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving with PagedAttention", SOSP 2023.** The vLLM paper. Read it for why memory, not compute, limits the batch, and how paging the KV cache fixes the padded-cache waste you just built.
- **Amey Agrawal et al., "Taming Throughput-Latency Tradeoff in LLM Inference with Sarathi-Serve", OSDI 2024** (and the earlier SARATHI paper on piggybacking decodes onto chunked prefills, 2023). Chunked prefill and stall-free scheduling, with careful measurements of the prefill-decode interference you saw in Break It.
- **Yinmin Zhong et al., "DistServe: Disaggregating Prefill and Decoding for Goodput-optimized Large Language Model Serving", OSDI 2024.** Defines goodput under TTFT and ITL SLOs, then takes the opposite approach to chunking: run prefill and decode on different GPUs.
