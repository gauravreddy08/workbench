## The Problem

You log every click on your ranker's results into a table: who clicked, which document, for which query, how long they stayed. After a few months it has 5 million rows. You write the obvious query for a debugging dashboard:

```sql
SELECT * FROM clicks WHERE user_id = 4242;
```

It takes 200 ms. Fine, until the dashboard runs it for 50 users per page load, and now each page takes 10 seconds. Someone on the team says "just add an index", you run one line, and the same query takes 0.1 ms. Two thousand times faster, from a line of SQL that took one second to run.

That's suspicious. Nothing about the data changed. The CPU didn't get faster. So where did 199.9 ms go? And if indexes are this good, why doesn't the database just index every column for you?

It doesn't, because there's a bill. A week later the ingestion job that writes those clicks has slowed to half its speed, because someone added five indexes "to be safe". Same trade, other side.

By the end of this lesson you'll know what an index is on disk, roughly byte for byte, and you'll be able to predict both numbers above before running anything.

<Origin year="1970" title="Too big for memory, too slow to search">

In the late 1960s, Rudolf Bayer and Edward McCreight were at Boeing's research labs, working on a plain problem: files of records too big to fit in memory, stored on disks where every random read meant physically moving an arm and waiting for the platter to spin round, tens of milliseconds each time. A sorted file let you binary search, but binary search on a million records means about 20 jumps, and 20 jumps on that disk was most of a second. Keeping the file sorted as new records arrived meant rewriting huge parts of it.

Their 1970 paper, "Organization and Maintenance of Large Ordered Indices", described a tree in which every node is one disk block, packed with hundreds of keys, so the tree is very wide and very short. Search takes a handful of block reads, and inserts only ever rewrite a few blocks. Nobody is sure what the B stands for (Boeing, balanced, broad, Bayer); the authors never settled it. By 1979 Douglas Comer's survey was titled "The Ubiquitous B-Tree", and it still is. Postgres, MySQL, SQLite, SQL Server, Oracle and most filesystems use a variant of it today.

</Origin>

## The Idea

### A phone book, and what the machine does instead

Imagine a box of 5 million index cards, one per click, in the order they happened. To find all of user 4242's clicks you have to look at every card. That's a <Term w="full table scan">Reading every row of a table and checking each one against the condition. Postgres calls it a Seq Scan (sequential scan). Cost grows with the size of the table, not the size of the answer.</Term>, and its cost has nothing to do with how many cards you want. Finding one card or a thousand, you read all 5 million.

A phone book is the fix everyone knows. Names are sorted, so you open near the middle, see "M", jump back toward "G", and land on the right page in a few flips. Two things make that work: the entries are **sorted by the thing you search for**, and you can **jump** to the middle of the book instead of reading from the front.

But your clicks can only be sorted one way, and you want to search by user, by document, by time. So you don't sort the box. You build a second, small box next to it: one card per click, holding only `user_id` and a note saying where the real card is ("box position 1,844,210"). Sort the small box by `user_id`. Now you binary search the small box, read the notes, and pull exactly the real cards you need.

That small sorted box is an <Term w="index">A separate data structure, stored alongside a table, that holds copies of one or more columns in sorted order (or hashed), each with a pointer to the row it came from. It exists only to make lookups fast.</Term>. That is the whole idea. Everything else in this lesson is about how to make the small box fast on a real disk, and what it costs to keep it sorted.

### What's actually on disk

Postgres stores a table as a file chopped into <Term w="pages">Fixed-size blocks that a database reads and writes as one unit. 8 KB in Postgres, 16 KB in MySQL's InnoDB, 4 KB by default in SQLite. The database never reads less than one page.</Term> of 8 KB. Rows go into pages in roughly the order they arrive, wherever there's space. This file is called the <Term w="heap">In Postgres, the table's main file: rows stored in pages in no particular order. Nothing to do with the heap data structure, or with your process's heap from the stack-and-heap lesson. It means "a pile".</Term>, meaning "a pile", nothing more. Every row has an address: which page, and which slot inside the page. Postgres calls that address the <Term w="ctid">A row's physical address in Postgres: (page number, slot number). For example (1844, 3) means slot 3 of page 1,844. Indexes store this to point at rows.</Term>, written like `(1844,3)`.

An index on `user_id` is a different file. Its entries are `(user_id, ctid)` pairs, kept in `user_id` order, also stored in 8 KB pages.

Two things to notice already.

**The index is a copy.** It duplicates the `user_id` column, plus 6 bytes of address and a few bytes of bookkeeping per row. On the 5-million-row table you'll build, the table is 365 MB and the primary-key index alone is 107 MB. Copies take space, and copies have to be kept in sync. Hold onto that; it's the whole write-cost story later.

**Sorted isn't enough.** If the index were one long sorted file of 13,000 pages, you could binary search it: about log₂(13,000) ≈ 14 jumps, each a separate page read. From [How slow is slow?](/lessons/latency-numbers/), a random SSD read is around 80 µs, so 14 of them is about 1.1 ms, and on Bayer's spinning disk with ~10 ms seeks, 140 ms. Better than a full scan, but each jump only rules out half of what's left. We can do much better by ruling out 99.7% per jump.

### B-trees: make every jump count

Here's the trick. Reading one page costs the same whether you use 8 bytes of it or all 8,192. So when you read a page, make it carry as much *direction* as possible.

Take the sorted index and cut it into pages of a few hundred entries each. Those are the bottom layer, the <Term w="leaf pages">The bottom level of a B-tree. Leaves hold the actual index entries (key plus row pointer), in sorted order, and each leaf links to the next one.</Term>. Now make a page that lists, for each leaf, the first key in it and the leaf's page number: "keys from 1 to 13,580: page 1; from 13,581: page 2; ...". That's a signpost page, an <Term w="inner page">A B-tree page above the leaves. It holds only separator keys and pointers to child pages, like the letter tabs on a phone book. Also called an internal or branch node.</Term>. If one signpost page can't hold all the leaves, make several, and a signpost page above *them*. Keep going until one page sits at the top: the <Term w="root">The single page at the top of a B-tree. Every search starts here.</Term>.

That's a <Term w="B+tree">The B-tree variant every major database uses: inner pages hold only separator keys for steering, all entries live in the leaves, and the leaves are chained left to right so you can walk them in order.</Term>. To find a key, read the root, pick the child whose range covers your key, read it, repeat until you're at a leaf. One page per level.

### Why it's always about four levels

The number of children one inner page can point to is its <Term w="fan-out">How many children one B-tree page points to. Roughly page size divided by the size of one entry. High fan-out means a short tree.</Term>. Let's work it out for the primary key of your clicks table, a `bigint` (8 bytes):

- One leaf entry in Postgres is the 8-byte key, plus an 8-byte header that includes the 6-byte row address, plus a 4-byte slot in the page's directory. Call it **20 bytes**.
- A page is 8,192 bytes, minus a few dozen of header. Postgres fills leaves to 90% when it builds an index, so about 8,150 × 0.9 / 20 ≈ **367 entries per leaf**. Inner entries are about the same size, so the fan-out is also in the high 300s.
- 5,000,000 rows / 367 ≈ 13,600 leaf pages. That's 13,600 × 8 KB = 107 MB, which is exactly the size Postgres reports for this index. The arithmetic is real.
- 13,600 leaves / 367 ≈ 37 inner pages. 37 fits in one root. **Height: 3.**

Each extra level multiplies capacity by ~367. Three levels hold about 367³ ≈ 49 million keys. Four levels: 18 billion. That's why the answer to "how deep is the index?" is almost always 2 to 4, whether the table has a thousand rows or a billion.

Now turn that into time, using the latency ladder. Each level is one page read.

- **All from SSD, nothing cached:** 3 or 4 reads × ~80 µs ≈ 0.3 ms.
- **Realistically:** the root and second level are tiny (one page, then a few dozen), get touched by every query, and so stay in RAM, either in Postgres's own cache or the OS <Term w="page cache">The kernel's copy of recently used file data in spare RAM. Covered in the files-and-disks lesson. A "disk read" that hits it costs microseconds, not tens of microseconds.</Term>. A cold lookup costs maybe 1 or 2 real SSD reads, plus one for the table page. Well under a millisecond.
- **All in RAM:** a few microseconds, mostly binary searching inside each page.

Compare a full scan of a 5 GB table at ~3 GB/s: well over a second. Play with the numbers. Push rows to 10 billion. Make the key 256 bytes (a long text column). Switch to 16 KB pages like MySQL.

### Keeping it sorted: inserts and page splits

Searching is the easy half. The hard half is that new rows keep arriving, and the leaves must stay sorted.

To insert key 42: walk down exactly as for a search, to the leaf where 42 belongs, and slide it into place inside that page. Most of the time the page has room and that's it: one page changed.

When the leaf is full, the database performs a <Term w="page split">When a B-tree page is full and needs one more entry: allocate a new page, move the upper half of the entries into it, and add a separator key pointing at the new page to the parent.</Term>. Take a new empty page, move the top half of the entries into it, link it into the leaf chain, and tell the parent: "keys from here up now live in the new page", which means inserting one separator key into the parent. If the parent is full too, it splits, and pushes a separator into *its* parent. In the worst case the split climbs all the way up, the root splits, and a new root is made above it.

That last step is the clever part. **A B-tree grows at the top, not the bottom.** Every leaf is always at the same depth, so the tree is always perfectly balanced, and no search is ever unlucky. Nobody has to rebalance anything.

Try it below. The pages here hold 3 or 4 keys so splits happen often. Insert 22, then 23, then 24. The first splits a leaf; the third splits a leaf, which overflows its parent, which splits the root, and a new root appears: every leaf is now one level deeper at once. Then search for keys and compare the two counters: the tree reads one page per level, the scan reads leaf pages from the left until it finds the key.

Two things the toy shows that are true of the real thing:

1. **The search cost is the height, and the height barely moves.** With 3 keys per page you'll reach height 4 at around 40 keys; with real pages of ~367 keys, height 4 holds tens of billions of rows.
2. **Pages are often not full.** A split leaves two half-full pages. After random inserts, B-tree pages average about 70% full (a classic result, and what you'll measure in Postgres in Break It). The "+5 in order" button shows the opposite pattern: ever-increasing keys always land in the rightmost leaf. This toy splits 50/50, so ascending inserts leave a trail of half-empty leaves. Real databases detect this case and split the rightmost page unevenly, keeping the old page full, which is why an auto-incrementing key gives you a compact index.

### The other half of the lookup: back to the table

The index leaf gives you a `ctid`, not the row. To return `SELECT *`, Postgres must still read the table page the row lives on. So "find one row by primary key" is really 3 index pages plus 1 table page, which is exactly what you'll see in `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)`: `Buffers: shared hit=1 read=3`, four 8 KB pages, one of which (the root) was already cached.

If the query matches many rows and they're scattered across the table, that's one table page per row. Fifty-six clicks for user 4242 means fifty-six different table pages, because that user's clicks arrived at fifty-six different times and landed wherever there was space. Hold that thought: it's the reason the planner sometimes refuses your index.

### Clustered vs secondary indexes

There are two ways to arrange the table and its indexes, and the databases you'll meet disagree.

**Postgres: a heap plus secondary indexes.** The table is the unordered pile. *Every* index, the primary key included, is a separate B-tree whose leaves point at `ctid`s. That makes every index a <Term w="secondary index">An index stored separately from the table, whose leaf entries point at where the row lives. A table can have many.</Term>.

**MySQL InnoDB, SQLite, SQL Server (usually): a clustered index.** The table *is* a B-tree, keyed by the primary key, and its leaves hold the entire row. That's a <Term w="clustered index">An index whose leaves contain the full rows, so the table itself is stored sorted by that key. A table can have only one, because rows can only be physically sorted one way.</Term>. Rows are physically sorted by primary key, so a primary-key range scan reads consecutive pages, with no jumping. Secondary indexes on that table store the *primary key value* instead of a physical address, so looking up by a secondary index means two descents: one down the secondary index to find the primary key, another down the clustered index to find the row.

| | Postgres (heap) | InnoDB (clustered) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where rows live | Heap pages, arrival order | Leaves of the primary-key B-tree |
| What an index leaf points to | Physical address `(page, slot)` | The primary key value |
| PK lookup | Index descent + 1 heap page | One descent, row is in the leaf |
| Secondary lookup | Index descent + 1 heap page | Secondary descent + PK descent |
| Row moves (update, reorganise) | New row version, indexes get new entries | Secondary indexes unaffected |

Neither is better, but it matters when you choose primary keys: on InnoDB, a random key like a UUID scatters inserts across the whole table, and every secondary index carries a copy of that 16-byte key.

In Postgres, if rows happen to be stored roughly in key order, an index range scan reads neighbouring table pages anyway. Postgres measures this per column as <Term w="correlation">A statistic Postgres keeps per column, from -1 to 1: how closely the physical order of rows matches the sorted order of that column. 1 means sorted on disk. Visible in the pg_stats view.</Term>. Your `created_at` column will have correlation near 1, because clicks are inserted in time order. `user_id` will be near 0.

### Covering indexes: skip the table entirely

If every column the query needs is already in the index, why visit the table at all? A <Term w="covering index">An index that contains every column a particular query needs, so the database can answer from the index alone without visiting the table.</Term> lets Postgres do an <Term w="index-only scan">A plan that reads only index pages. In Postgres it still checks a small visibility map to know whether each row is visible to this transaction; after VACUUM, almost no table pages are touched.</Term>.

You can make one two ways. Put the extra columns in the key, `(user_id, dwell_ms)`, or carry them as passengers that aren't part of the sort order: `CREATE INDEX ... ON clicks (user_id) INCLUDE (dwell_ms)`. On your table, averaging `dwell_ms` over 1,000 users (50,000 rows) took 66 ms with a plain index on `user_id`, reading 30,846 table pages, and 2.9 ms with the covering index, reading about 140 index pages and zero table pages.

The catch is the one from before: the index is a copy, and now a bigger one.

### Composite indexes and the leftmost-prefix rule

A <Term w="composite index">An index on several columns at once, sorted by the first column, then by the second within ties of the first, and so on. Also called a multi-column index.</Term> on `(user_id, created_at)` is sorted like a phone book sorted by last name, then first name. All of user 12's entries are together, in time order; then all of user 88's; and so on.

That ordering decides which queries it helps, and it is the most useful single fact about indexes in practice.

The general rule is the <Term w="leftmost-prefix rule">A composite index on (a, b, c) can be used efficiently for conditions on a, on a and b, or on a, b and c: any prefix of its columns from the left. A condition on b alone can't use the sort order, because b is only sorted within each value of a.</Term>. An index on `(a, b, c)` is sorted by `a` first, so it helps queries that pin down `a`; or `a` and `b`; or all three. It's nearly useless for `b` alone, the way a phone book sorted by last name doesn't help you find everyone called "Wei".

A good recipe for ordering columns:

1. Columns compared with `=` go first.
2. Then at most one column used for a range (`>`, `BETWEEN`) or for `ORDER BY`. After a range, later columns can't narrow the search, because within the range they aren't sorted.
3. Then, optionally, `INCLUDE` the columns you only read, to make it covering.

For the dashboard query, `WHERE user_id = ? AND created_at >= ? ORDER BY created_at LIMIT 20`, that gives `(user_id, created_at)`. The index even hands rows back already in `created_at` order, so the sort and the `LIMIT` are free: Postgres reads 20 entries and stops.

### Selectivity, and why the planner ignores your index

Postgres doesn't run the first plan it thinks of. Its <Term w="query planner">The part of the database that considers several ways to run a query (scan the table, use this index, use that one, in what order to join) and picks the one its cost model says is cheapest. Also called the optimizer.</Term> estimates the cost of each option and picks the cheapest. It estimates using statistics gathered by `ANALYZE`: for each column, the most common values and how often they appear, a histogram of the rest, the number of distinct values, and the correlation.

The key number is <Term w="selectivity">The fraction of rows a condition matches. user_id = 4242 matches 56 of 5M rows (0.001%, very selective). country = 'US' matches 39% (not selective at all).</Term>. If a condition matches a tiny fraction of rows, the index wins by a mile. If it matches a big fraction, the index loses, for a reason you can now work out yourself:

- A sequential scan reads the 46,729 table pages **in order**, which disks and the OS do at full speed (readahead, big requests).
- An index scan reads index pages **plus one table page per matching row**, in random order. If 39% of rows match and they're spread evenly, nearly *every* page has a match. You end up reading the whole table anyway, in random order, plus the index.

Postgres prices a random page read at 4x a sequential one by default (`random_page_cost = 4`, `seq_page_cost = 1`), a number chosen in the spinning-disk era. Somewhere around a few percent of rows, depending on row width and correlation, the lines cross and it switches to a scan.

Between "a few rows" and "most rows" there's a middle option, the <Term w="bitmap scan">A two-step plan: first scan the index and collect the addresses of all matching rows into a bitmap of table pages, then read those pages in physical order. Turns random table reads into sorted ones. Appears as Bitmap Index Scan + Bitmap Heap Scan.</Term>, which collects all the matching addresses first, sorts them by page, and then visits each needed page once, in order.

There's a subtle trap here that your measurements will show. On your table, `country = 'FR'` matches only 5.6% of rows, and the planner used a bitmap scan. It still read every one of the 46,729 table pages, because with about 107 rows per page, almost every page holds at least one French click. **Selectivity in rows isn't what matters. Selectivity in pages is.**

So, the usual reasons Postgres ignores your index:

1. **The condition isn't selective enough** (above). The planner is usually right.
2. **The query wraps the column in something.** `WHERE lower(email) = ...`, `WHERE user_id + 0 = 4242`, `WHERE created_at::date = ...`. The index is sorted by the column, not by `lower(column)`. Fix: write the condition on the bare column, or build an <Term w="expression index">An index on the result of an expression, like lower(email), rather than on a raw column. Postgres can then use it for queries that contain that exact expression.</Term>.
3. **The leftmost column isn't constrained.** A query on `created_at` alone can't use `(user_id, created_at)` for seeking.
4. **The table is tiny.** If it fits in a few pages, reading them all is cheaper than a descent.
5. **The statistics are stale or wrong.** After a big load, until `ANALYZE` runs (autovacuum does it eventually), the planner may think the table is empty or the value rare. Correlated columns fool it too, because it assumes conditions are independent.
6. **The cost settings don't match your hardware.** On SSDs, random reads aren't 4x worse. Many people set `random_page_cost` to around 1.1. Change it if you measure that the index plan really is faster, not before.

### The bill: what every index costs on write

Now the other side. When you `INSERT` a row into a table with five indexes, Postgres:

1. Writes the row into a heap page.
2. For **each** index: descends from the root to the right leaf and inserts an entry, possibly splitting pages.
3. Records all of those page changes in the <Term w="WAL">Write-ahead log. Postgres writes every change to this append-only log, and fsyncs it at commit, before changing data pages on disk. After a crash it replays the log. The files-and-disks lesson is the background.</Term>, so they can be replayed after a crash.

So one row becomes six B-tree or heap insertions, and if the indexed values are random, each lands on a different leaf, probably one not in cache. An `UPDATE` is worse than you'd guess: Postgres never overwrites a row in place; it writes a new version of the row elsewhere, so every index needs a new entry pointing at it too. (There's an optimization, <Term w="HOT update">Heap-only tuple update. If no indexed column changed and the new row version fits on the same page, Postgres skips adding new index entries. One reason not to index columns you update constantly.</Term>, that skips the index work when no indexed column changed and the new version fits on the same page.)

This gives you **write amplification**: one logical write causes many physical ones. Indexes also cost space, cache (index pages compete with table pages for RAM), and `VACUUM` time.

### When writes dominate: LSM-trees

A B-tree updates data **in place**: find the page, change it, write it back. Each insert is a small random write, and with many indexes and random keys, that's a lot of random I/O. Some systems take in far more writes than reads: event logs, metrics, message stores, the click log itself at large scale. For them, there's a different design.

Remember the trick from [Why not a JSON file?](/lessons/why-databases/): appending to a log is the cheapest write there is. The <Term w="LSM-tree">Log-structured merge-tree. A storage design that buffers writes in a sorted in-memory table, flushes it to disk as immutable sorted files, and merges those files in the background. Used by RocksDB, LevelDB, Cassandra, ScyllaDB, HBase.</Term> (log-structured merge-tree) builds a whole index out of that idea.

1. **Write:** append the change to a log on disk (for crash safety), then insert it into the <Term w="memtable">The in-memory, sorted part of an LSM-tree, usually a skip list or balanced tree. All new writes go here first. Typically tens of megabytes.</Term>, a sorted structure in RAM. That's it. No disk page was read or rewritten.
2. **Flush:** when the memtable reaches some size (RocksDB's default is 64 MB), write it to disk in one sequential pass as an <Term w="SSTable">Sorted string table. An immutable file of key-value pairs in sorted order, with a small index of where each block starts. Written once, never modified, eventually merged away.</Term>: a sorted, immutable file. Start a fresh memtable.
3. **Compact:** in the background, merge SSTables together (like the merge step of merge sort), keeping only the newest version of each key and dropping deleted ones. That's <Term w="compaction">Background merging of an LSM-tree's sorted files into fewer, larger ones, discarding overwritten and deleted entries. Costs disk bandwidth, keeps reads fast.</Term>. Files are organized into levels, each roughly 10x bigger than the last.

Reads are where you pay. A key might be in the memtable or in any of the files, so a lookup may have to check several places, newest first. Two things keep that tolerable. Each file has a small sparse index, so checking one file costs at most one block read. And each file has a <Term w="bloom filter">A compact bit array that answers "is this key in the set?" with either "definitely not" or "maybe". With about 10 bits per key it says "maybe" wrongly about 1% of the time. Lets a reader skip files that can't contain the key.</Term>, so most files that don't contain the key are skipped without touching the disk at all.

The trade, roughly:

| | B-tree | LSM-tree |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Insert | Random page writes, in place | Sequential appends; compaction rewrites data later |
| Point read | One descent, ~1 cold read | Memtable + bloom checks, usually ~1 file read |
| Range read | Walk the leaves | Merge across files |
| Space | Pages ~70% full | Compact, but old versions linger until compaction |
| Hidden cost | Page splits, fragmentation | Compaction uses disk bandwidth in the background |

Rule of thumb: read-heavy with updates to existing rows, B-tree. Ingest-heavy, append-mostly, LSM. Postgres and MySQL are B-tree databases. RocksDB is an LSM engine embedded in many systems (including as a MySQL storage engine), and Cassandra and ScyllaDB are LSM databases.

### Hash indexes

Last, the simplest one. If you only ever ask "is key X here, and where?", you don't need sorting at all. A <Term w="hash index">An index that hashes each key to a bucket, giving roughly one-step equality lookups. It has no order, so it can't do ranges, sorting, or prefix searches.</Term> hashes the key to a bucket and looks there. It's the dict of indexes: O(1) lookups, no order.

Postgres has them (`CREATE INDEX ... USING hash`, crash-safe since version 10), but they only support `=`, and a B-tree descent is already only 3 or 4 mostly-cached pages, so B-tree stays the default. Hash indexes shine in memory: Redis is essentially a giant hash table, and the Bitcask design keeps a hash table of key → file offset in RAM over an append-only log, which is exactly the store you build in [Why not a JSON file?](/lessons/why-databases/).

<Task id="t5" title="Write a B+tree" agent="hands">

In plain Python, no libraries beyond `bisect`. This is the core of the lesson; don't let an agent write it. Here's the shape:

```python
# bptree.py
from bisect import bisect_left, bisect_right

class Leaf:
    __slots__ = ("keys", "vals", "next")      # next: the leaf to the right, or None

class Inner:
    __slots__ = ("keys", "kids")              # len(kids) == len(keys) + 1

class BPlusTree:
    def __init__(self, order: int = 64):
        """order = max keys per node. Start with a single empty leaf as the root."""

    def _find_leaf(self, key) -> tuple[Leaf, list[Inner]]:
        """Descend from the root. Return the leaf where key belongs, and the path of
        inner nodes above it (you'll need the path to push splits upward)."""

    def search(self, key):
        """Return the value for key, or None."""

    def insert(self, key, val) -> None:
        """Insert or overwrite. If the leaf overflows, split it and push a separator
        into the parent; repeat upward; if the root splits, make a new root."""

    def range(self, lo, hi):
        """Yield (key, val) for lo <= key < hi, in order, using the leaf chain."""

    def height(self) -> int: ...
```

Hints, in the order you'll need them:

- In an inner node, child `i` holds keys in `[keys[i-1], keys[i])`. So the child to follow for `key` is `kids[bisect_right(node.keys, key)]`. Get this wrong by one and keys equal to a separator go missing.
- **Leaf split:** move the upper half of keys and vals to a new leaf, fix the `next` pointers, and send the new leaf's *first key* up as the separator. The key stays in the leaf; the parent gets a copy.
- **Inner split:** the middle key moves *up* and is removed from both halves. That asymmetry with leaf splits is the most common bug.
- Start with `order=3` so splits happen constantly, and print the tree level by level after each insert. Compare with Figure 004 using the same inserts.

Then test it against something you trust:

```python
import random, bisect
def check(order: int, n: int = 200_000) -> None:
    t, ref = BPlusTree(order), {}
    for k in random.sample(range(10_000_000), n):
        t.insert(k, k * 2); ref[k] = k * 2
    s = sorted(ref)
    # 1. every key found with the right value; a missing key returns None
```

What the numbers should tell you:

**Scan vs index: about 2,000x.** The scan read 46,729 pages to return 56 rows; the index read 3 index pages and 56 table pages. That ~790x gap in pages explains most of it; the rest is CPU spent checking 5 million rows. Three processes made the scan 3x faster, but no parallelism closes a 790x gap in work.

**The primary key lookup is 4 pages: 3 index levels and 1 table page.** That matches the height arithmetic exactly, and `bt_metap` should report root level 2.

**The US query is the honest surprise.** The planner chose the scan, but forcing the index was a little *faster* on my Mac (86 ms vs 112 ms). Everything was already in RAM (365 MB fits easily in the page cache of a 48 GB machine), so "random" reads cost the same as sequential ones and `random_page_cost = 4` was too pessimistic. On a cold cache or slower disk the planner would be right. This is exactly when people lower `random_page_cost` on SSD machines. Check whether your run agrees.

**The FR query reads every page despite matching 5.6% of rows,** because the matches are spread across all pages. Pages, not rows.

**Composite order: 23 vs 7,322 pages for the same query.** With `(user_id, created_at)` the matches are contiguous and the `LIMIT 20` stops after 20 entries. With `(created_at, user_id)` Postgres walks every entry since June 1 checking `user_id`, and fetches table pages as it goes. Flip to the daily report and the winner flips too: 70 pages vs a full scan. Indexes are designed for queries, not for columns.

**Covering: 66 ms to 2.9 ms** by not visiting 30,846 table pages.

**Writes: the primary key is nearly free, random-key indexes aren't.** Ascending keys (`id`, `created_at`) append to the rightmost leaf. Random keys (`user_id`, `doc_id`, `query`) touch a different leaf each time. Bulk inserts got 11x slower with five indexes; single-row inserts only 2x slower, because each one also pays a commit. (On macOS, Postgres's default commit doesn't force the SSD's own cache to flush, which is why one-row commits are so cheap here; see [Files, disks and fsync](/lessons/files-and-disks/). Expect fewer rows per second on Linux with a real flush.)

**Your B+tree vs a sorted list: 6 s vs 280 s.** `insort` finds the spot with a fast binary search but then has to shift every element after it one slot to the right. On average that's a quarter of a million pointers, about 2 MB of memory moved per insert, around 2 TB in total. That is exactly why databases don't keep one big sorted array on disk: each insert would rewrite half the file. The B+tree only ever shifts entries inside one small node. And the `dict` beats both by 40x, because hashing doesn't keep anything in order. That's the hash index trade, measured.

## Break It

**1. Random primary keys.** Create three tables with a two-column schema (`id`, `v int`) and insert 3 million rows into each: `id` as ascending `bigint`; as `(random() * 1e15)::bigint`; as `uuid` from `gen_random_uuid()`. Time each and compare index sizes. On my Mac: 9.5 s and 64 MB; 29 s and 79 MB; 96 s and 123 MB. Then `REINDEX` the random-bigint table and check again (it shrank to 64 MB). The random keys produced pages about 73% full on average instead of 90%, from splits all over the tree, and the UUID index is bigger again because each key is 16 bytes. Every insert dirties a different leaf, and Postgres writes a full copy of each page into the WAL the first time it changes after a checkpoint, so random keys also multiply WAL volume. This is why people use time-ordered IDs (UUIDv7, snowflake IDs) for big tables.

**2. Stale statistics.** Create a new table, insert 1 million rows where every `country` is `'US'`, run `ANALYZE`, then insert 1 million `'FR'` rows and immediately `EXPLAIN` a query for `'FR'` before autovacuum notices. Watch the estimated rows (`rows=`) differ wildly from the actual. Run `ANALYZE` and see the plan change. Most "Postgres randomly picked a terrible plan" stories are this.

**3. Break your B+tree on purpose.** Change the inner-node split to keep the middle key in the right half (as the leaf split does) instead of moving it up. Run your checker. Which test fails first, and why does search still work for most keys? Then put the off-by-one in `_find_leaf` (use `bisect_left`) and find which keys go missing. Your checker should catch both; if it doesn't, it isn't checking enough.

**4. Index everything.** Index every column of `clicks` and rerun the bulk insert. Compare `pg_indexes_size('clicks')` to the table size (indexes bigger than the table is common), then check `pg_stat_user_indexes.idx_scan` after your dashboard queries: most were never used once, and every one was paid for on every insert.

## Explain It Back

Answer out loud, in plain words, as if in an interview. Then check.

<details>
<summary>1. What is a database index, physically? Why does it make reads fast?</summary>

A separate structure next to the table, usually a B+tree, stored in the same fixed-size pages as the table. It holds a copy of the indexed columns in sorted order, each with a pointer to where the row lives. Without it, finding rows means reading every page of the table. With it, the database descends the tree from the root: each page it reads holds hundreds of separator keys, so each read narrows the search a few-hundred-fold, and it reaches the right leaf in 3 or 4 page reads even for billions of rows. Then it follows the pointer to the one table page with the row. You go from reading the whole table to reading about 4 pages.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. How does a B-tree lookup scale from a million rows to ten billion? Show the arithmetic.</summary>

The fan-out is about page size divided by entry size: an 8 KB page with ~20-byte entries for a bigint key holds roughly 370 entries. Height is about log base 370 of the row count. A million rows: ~2,700 leaves, 8 inner pages, a root: height 3. Ten billion: 27 million leaves, then 74,000 inner pages, then 200, then the root: height 4. So 10,000x more data costs one more page read. The top levels are small and hot, so they're in RAM; a cold lookup is about one or two SSD reads, ~100-200 µs. What does scale linearly is the index's size on disk (one entry per row, ~20 bytes plus slack, so roughly 200 GB at ten billion), its insert cost in total, and any full scan.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. You added an index and Postgres still does a sequential scan. Give four possible reasons and how you'd check each.</summary>

(1) The condition matches too many rows, or rather too many pages, so reading the table in order is cheaper; check the estimated vs actual rows in `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` and the value's frequency in `pg_stats`. (2) The query wraps the column in a function, cast or arithmetic (`lower(email)`, `created_at::date`), so the sorted order doesn't apply; rewrite the condition or build an expression index. (3) It's a composite index and the query doesn't constrain the leftmost column. (4) The statistics are stale (fresh bulk load, no `ANALYZE` yet), or the cost settings assume spinning disks; run `ANALYZE`, and if `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` with `enable_seqscan = off` is consistently faster, consider lowering `random_page_cost`. Also: the table is tiny, so a scan is genuinely cheaper.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Your ingestion job got 2x slower after someone added indexes. Explain why and what you'd do.</summary>

Every insert writes the row and then one entry into every index: descend each B-tree, insert into a leaf, sometimes split pages, and log all of it to the WAL. Indexes on random values (user IDs, UUIDs, text) put each insert on a different leaf page, often not in cache, so they cost far more than indexes on ascending values like an id or timestamp. Updates are expensive too: Postgres writes a new row version, so every index gets a new entry unless it's a HOT update. What I'd do: drop indexes `pg_stat_user_indexes` shows nobody scans; merge overlapping ones (`(user_id, created_at)` makes `(user_id)` redundant); for big backfills, drop secondary indexes, load, and rebuild them in one sorted pass; prefer time-ordered keys. If the workload is overwhelmingly writes, an LSM-tree store is built for exactly that.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. B-tree vs LSM-tree: what's the trade, in one minute?</summary>

A B-tree keeps one sorted structure on disk and updates pages in place: reads are one predictable descent, but each write is a small random write somewhere in the tree. An LSM-tree never updates in place: writes go to a log and a sorted in-memory table, which is flushed as an immutable sorted file when full; background compaction merges files into bigger sorted levels. Writes become cheap sequential appends, but a read may need to check the memtable and several files (bloom filters skip most of them), and compaction keeps rewriting data in the background, using disk bandwidth. B-tree for read-heavy and update-heavy; LSM for write-heavy ingestion.

</details>

## Go Deeper

- **Martin Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, Chapter 3: "Storage and Retrieval".** The best single explanation of hash indexes, SSTables, LSM-trees and B-trees side by side, and where this lesson's LSM section comes from. Read it next.
- **Markus Winand, [Use The Index, Luke!](https://use-the-index-luke.com/)** A free online book about indexing for developers. The chapters on the leaf chain, composite index column order and "functions in the where clause" are the practical companion to this lesson.
- **PostgreSQL docs: [Chapter 11, Indexes](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/indexes.html) and [Using EXPLAIN](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/using-explain.html).** Covers multicolumn indexes, index-only scans, expression indexes, and how to read every line of a plan. The B-tree chapter also explains deduplication.
- **Douglas Comer, "The Ubiquitous B-Tree", ACM Computing Surveys, 1979.** The classic survey: B-trees, B+trees, why they won. Short and readable.
- **Patrick O'Neil, Edward Cheng, Dieter Gawlick and Elizabeth O'Neil, "The Log-Structured Merge-Tree (LSM-Tree)", Acta Informatica, 1996.** The original LSM paper. Read the introduction for the cost argument; it's still the clearest statement of why batching writes wins.
- **Alex Petrov, _Database Internals_ (O'Reilly, 2019), Part I.** Goes deep on B-tree page layouts, splits, merges, and LSM compaction strategies, with diagrams. The book to read if you want to build a storage engine.
- **CMU 15-445/645 Database Systems (Andy Pavlo), the B+tree and index lectures.** Free on YouTube. Fast, opinionated, and full of what real systems actually do.
- **The Postgres source: `src/backend/access/nbtree/README`.** How Postgres's B-tree handles many readers and writers at once (the Lehman and Yao design). Read it once your own B+tree works and you wonder what happens when two inserts split the same page.
