This is how today's reasoning models learn: the model tries the same problem several times, a small piece of code checks which attempts were right, and training nudges the model toward whatever it did on the right ones. Nobody writes example answers for it to copy. GRPO, short for Group Relative Policy Optimization, is the popular recipe for doing that, and at small scale it runs on your Spark.

It's the method behind DeepSeek-R1 and much of the wave of models that "think" before answering, and it's the training half of the coding-RL work Anthropic hires for. It also ties straight back to your inference work. Most of a training run is spent waiting for the model to write its attempts, one token at a time, so every speedup you've chased on the Spark while benchmarking against Fireworks becomes, here, training speed.

It builds on [What is an RL environment?](/lessons/rl-environments/) for the practice arena and [Rewards and verifiers](/lessons/verifiers-rewards/) for the checker, and [Preference tuning with DPO](/lessons/dpo/) is the closest thing you've already implemented. ## The Problem

You have a checker. Give it a Countdown puzzle ("use 3, 7 and 4 exactly once to make 40") and a model's answer, and it says 1 or 0. It is fast, it is never tired, and it cannot be argued with: `(3 + 7) * 4` is 40 or it isn't.

You also have Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, which solves some of these puzzles and fumbles the rest. You want it to get better at them. So you reach for what you know.

**Supervised fine-tuning** needs worked solutions to copy. You don't have any. You could write a solver and generate them, but then the model learns to imitate your solver's style, not to search. And for your coding tasks, there's no solver to write.

**DPO** needs pairs: this answer is better than that one. Closer. You could sample two answers, check both, and keep pairs where one passed and one failed. That throws away most samples and still trains offline, on answers from an older version of the model.

What you actually have is simpler and stranger: a model that can *try*, and a checker that can *grade*. No target tokens. No labelled pairs. Just "that attempt scored 1, that one scored 0". Cross-entropy loss needs to know the right next token. Here there is no right next token, only a verdict at the very end.

Turning a verdict into a gradient is the whole problem. The answer is policy gradient, the trick that makes it practical at LLM scale is GRPO, and the reason it suddenly works in 2025 is that the checker is code.

<Origin year="2024" title="Dropping the critic">

RL for language models started with RLHF: train a reward model on human preferences, then optimise the LLM against it with PPO (OpenAI's InstructGPT, 2022). PPO came from robotics and games, and it brought a second network along: a *critic* that guesses, at every token, how well the answer is going to turn out. For a 7B policy, that's another 7B network to train and hold in memory, and it is hard to train well when the only reward arrives at the end of a 500-token answer.

In February 2024, a DeepSeek team training a math model (Shao et al., *DeepSeekMath*) replaced the critic with something blunt: sample a group of answers to the same question, and grade each one against the group's average. They called it Group Relative Policy Optimization. In January 2025, DeepSeek-R1 showed that GRPO plus rule-based rewards (is the final answer right? is the format right?) applied straight to a base model, with no supervised warm-up, made the model learn to write long chains of reasoning on its own. That variant, R1-Zero, went from 15.6% to 71.0% <Term w="pass@1">The fraction of problems solved when the model gets one attempt per problem (in practice averaged over several sampled attempts).</Term> on AIME 2024 according to the paper. Within months, labs and hobbyists were reproducing the effect on small models and toy tasks, often on a single machine.

</Origin>

## The Idea

### The setup, in RL words

You already met these words in [What is an RL environment?](/lessons/rl-environments/). Quickly, for an LLM:

The model is the <Term w="policy">Whatever decides what to do next. For an LLM, the policy is the model itself: given the text so far, it gives a probability for every possible next token.</Term>, written π<sub>θ</sub> (θ is its weights). A prompt is the situation. Each token it writes is an <Term w="action">One choice the policy makes. For an LLM, one sampled token. A whole completion is a sequence of actions.</Term>. When it's done, a verifier hands back a <Term w="reward">A number that says how good the outcome was. Here, usually 1 for correct and 0 for wrong, given once at the end of the completion.</Term>.

The goal: change θ so the *average* reward over prompts and sampled answers goes up. Not the reward of one answer; the expected reward, averaging over the randomness of sampling. That expectation is the thing we take a gradient of.

### Policy gradient, from scratch

Here's the scene. You ask the model one puzzle, sample four answers, and two of them pass. What should change?

Obviously: the two answers that passed should become more likely, and the two that failed less likely. The model wrote each answer token by token, and it knows exactly how probable each of its own answers was: the <Term w="log-probability">The log of the probability the model assigned to a sequence. For a completion, it's the sum over tokens of log P(token | everything before it). You get it from the same forward pass you'd use for cross-entropy.</Term> of the whole completion is just the sum of the log-probs of its tokens. So "make this answer more likely" has a direct meaning: take a gradient step that increases its log-prob.

Do that for every sampled answer, weighted by how good it was. That's the entire idea:

```text
gradient  ≈  average over samples of   (how good it was)  ×  ∇ log π_θ(answer | prompt)
```

This is **REINFORCE** (Williams, 1992), and the surprising part is that it's not a heuristic. It is an exact, unbiased estimate of the gradient of expected reward. The one-line reason: the gradient of an average over samples equals the average of (reward × gradient of log-probability), because `∇π = π ∇log π`. You can't differentiate through the act of sampling, or through a verifier that's a Python function, but you don't need to. You only differentiate the log-probs, which the model gives you for free.

If you've written cross-entropy training, look at the shape again. SFT on one answer is "increase log π(answer)". Policy gradient is the same thing with a weight in front, and the weight can be negative. **Policy gradient is SFT on your own samples, weighted by how good they were.**

In code, that's a loss you minimise:

```python
loss = -(weight * logprob_of_completion).mean()   # weight comes from the reward
```

### The noise problem, and baselines

Plain REINFORCE uses the raw reward as the weight. Here is why that hurts.

Suppose every reward is shifted up: the checker gives 6 for correct and 5 for wrong. The *best direction* hasn't changed; correct is still better than wrong by exactly 1. But now every answer has a large positive weight, so every sample says "make me more likely". The gradient still comes out right *on average*, because the pushes on wrong answers cancel against everyone else's pushes over many samples. But any single batch is a tug of war between huge forces with a small difference.

The fix is to subtract a <Term w="baseline">A number subtracted from the reward before using it as the weight. Anything that doesn't depend on which answer was sampled is allowed, and it leaves the gradient unbiased. A good baseline is "the reward you expected".</Term>: weight = reward − (what you expected to get). Answers better than expected get pushed up, worse ones pushed down, and an answer that's exactly as good as expected isn't pushed at all. That difference has a name, the <Term w="advantage">How much better this answer was than expected: reward minus a baseline. Positive means "do more of this", negative means "do less".</Term>.

### PPO in one paragraph

PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) makes two moves. First, a learned baseline: a <Term w="value model">A second network (often the same size as the policy) trained to predict, from any partial answer, the reward it will eventually get. Also called the critic. Its prediction is the baseline at every token.</Term> that predicts, at every token, the reward the answer will end up with, so each token's advantage is "how much better things went than the critic expected at this point" (computed with a method called <Term w="GAE">Generalized Advantage Estimation. A way to blend the critic's predictions at successive tokens into a per-token advantage, trading a little bias for much less variance.</Term>). Second, a safety rail: samples are generated by the policy as it was at the start of the step, but you take several gradient steps on them, so the policy drifts away from the one that produced the data. PPO measures that drift per token as a <Term w="probability ratio">π_new(token) / π_old(token): how much more (or less) likely the current policy makes a token than the policy that actually sampled it. 1.0 means unchanged.</Term> and *clips* it: once a token's probability has moved more than about 20% in the direction the advantage wants, its gradient switches off. For LLMs this means holding four models: the policy and the value model (both trained), plus a reward model and a frozen reference (both used for scoring).

The formula, for the record, is `min(ratio × A, clip(ratio, 1−ε, 1+ε) × A)` with ε around 0.2. The `min` makes it pessimistic: you get credit for improving a token only up to the clip, and never get rewarded for moving past it. Notice the right half of each plot: if the policy moved a token the *wrong* way (a good token became less likely), the gradient is still on, pulling it back.

<Predict id="clip-noop">

You generate a batch of samples, then do exactly **one** gradient step on them before generating fresh samples. What does the clip do during that step?

<Fragment slot="answer">

Nothing. The policy that generated the samples is the policy you're differentiating, so every ratio is exactly 1.0, nowhere near 0.8 or 1.2. The clipped objective's gradient at ratio 1 is just `A × ∇log π`: plain REINFORCE with a baseline. The clip only starts mattering when you reuse the same samples for several updates (TRL calls this `num_iterations`; the GRPO paper calls it μ), or when the sampler and trainer disagree slightly about probabilities, which happens when you generate with a separate inference engine. Keep this in mind when you implement it: with one update per batch, a bug in your clipping code is invisible.

</Fragment>
</Predict>

### GRPO: grade on a curve

The value model is the expensive, fragile part. It's a whole second LLM, and it has to learn to predict a 0-or-1 that only arrives after hundreds of tokens. GRPO asks: what if the baseline came from just... asking again?

For each prompt, sample a <Term w="group">G completions of the same prompt, sampled independently from the current policy. G is typically 4-64. The group is the unit that GRPO computes statistics over.</Term> of G answers. Score each with the verifier. Then the advantage of answer i is how it did compared with its siblings:

```text
A_i = (r_i − mean(r_1..r_G)) / std(r_1..r_G)
```

That's it. The group mean *is* the baseline: an estimate of "what this model usually gets on this prompt", made fresh, for this exact prompt, from the model as it is right now. No second network, no training it, no guessing per token. Every token of answer i gets the same advantage A<sub>i</sub>.

Dividing by the std is a separate choice, and a debated one (more on that in Break It). It puts every prompt's advantages on the same scale, so an easy prompt where 7/8 pass and a hard one where 1/8 pass both produce pushes of similar total size.

With 0/1 rewards the formula has a neat closed form. If k of G answers pass and p = k/G, then each correct answer gets A = √((1−p)/p) and each wrong one gets −√(p/(1−p)) (using the population std). One success in 16 tries: that answer gets +3.87, each failure −0.26. **The rarer a success, the harder GRPO reinforces it.** That's exactly what you want when the model has just stumbled onto something new.

Three things to find in the figure:

1. **Zero-spread groups are dead weight.** If all G answers pass, every advantage is 0. If all fail, every advantage is 0. No gradient flows from that prompt, but you still paid to generate all G answers. The chance of a dead group is `p^G + (1−p)^G`. I checked the formula against 200,000 simulated groups per setting and it matched to three decimals. With p = 0.05 and G = 8, **66% of groups are dead**; at G = 16 it's 44%.
2. **Bigger G rescues hard prompts.** More samples, more chances to stumble on one success, and that success gets a big push.
3. **Noise fakes a signal.** Add reward noise to an all-wrong group and the std is no longer zero, so the bars light up. With std normalization, a tiny noise std is divided by a tiny std and comes out full size. The model is now being trained hard on coin flips. This is why the reward has to be *verifiable*: exact, deterministic, not a judge's mood.

### The full GRPO loss

Put the pieces together. For each prompt q, sample G completions o<sub>1</sub>..o<sub>G</sub> from the old policy (the weights at the start of this step), compute A<sub>i</sub> from their rewards, then minimise:

```text
loss = − (1/G) Σ_i  (1/|o_i|) Σ_t  [ min( ρ_it · A_i,  clip(ρ_it, 1−ε, 1+ε) · A_i )  −  β · KL_it ]

ρ_it  = π_θ(o_it | q, o_i,<t) / π_old(o_it | q, o_i,<t)       (per-token ratio)
KL_it = π_ref/π_θ − log(π_ref/π_θ) − 1                       (per-token, ratios of that token's probs)
```

Read it as three ideas. The first term is PPO's clipped policy gradient, with the group advantage in place of the critic. The `1/|o_i|` averages over tokens within each answer (another debated choice). And the last term is a leash.

The leash is a <Term w="KL divergence">A measure of how different two probability distributions are. Zero when identical, larger as they drift apart. Here it measures how far the trained model's token probabilities have moved from the frozen starting model's.</Term> to a <Term w="reference model">A frozen copy of the model from before RL started. It never trains; it only answers "how likely would the original model have found this token?"</Term>. Without it, the policy is free to wander anywhere that scores well, including into degenerate text that happens to pass the verifier. β sets how short the leash is; DeepSeekMath used 0.04. The odd-looking formula is an estimator of KL that's always non-negative and needs only the probability of the sampled token, not the whole vocabulary (John Schulman's note "Approximating KL Divergence" explains it). Note that GRPO puts KL in the loss; PPO-style RLHF instead subtracts it from the reward. Some recent recipes (DAPO, and recent TRL defaults) drop the KL term entirely for verifiable-reward tasks. Check what your library does rather than assuming.

### Why verifiable rewards changed everything

RL on language models wasn't new in 2025. What changed was the reward.

A learned reward model (the RLHF kind) is a neural network trained on human judgments. Optimise against it hard and the policy finds its blind spots: longer answers, confident tone, flattering phrasing, whatever the reward model over-scores. This is <Term w="reward hacking">The policy finding a way to score high that isn't what you meant: exploiting a bug or blind spot in the reward rather than doing the task. Covered in depth in the verifiers lesson.</Term>, and with a neural reward model it sets a ceiling on how long and how hard you can train. The DeepSeek-R1 paper says outright that it avoided neural reward models for its reasoning RL for this reason.

A verifier for a math answer or a unit-test suite is different. It's cheap (microseconds to seconds), deterministic, and much harder to fool (not impossible; see [Rewards and verifiers](/lessons/verifiers-rewards/)). You can run millions of RL steps against it. AI2's Tülu 3 report (late 2024) gave the recipe its usual name: <Term w="RLVR">Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards. RL where the reward comes from a programmatic check (exact answer match, tests passing) rather than a learned model of human preference.</Term>.

Stated carefully, here is what DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) showed. Starting from their large base model (DeepSeek-V3-Base) and running GRPO with only rule-based rewards (answer accuracy, and a format reward for putting reasoning inside think tags), the model's average response length grew steadily over training, and it began to show behaviours like re-checking its work and trying alternative approaches. Nobody wrote examples of that; the reward made it pay. That R1-Zero model had problems (poor readability, mixing languages), so the released R1 used a small "cold start" supervised set first, then RL, then more supervised data and more RL. It was not the first reasoning model (OpenAI's o1 came out in September 2024 without a published method), but it was the first to publish a recipe this simple, and the simplicity is why everyone could try it.

What you should take from it at your scale: **RL with a verifier reinforces strategies the model can already sometimes find.** A 0.5B model will get better at Countdown. It will not discover algebra.

### What you watch

A supervised run has one number that matters: loss. A GRPO run's loss is nearly meaningless (with one update per batch and group-centred advantages, it hovers around zero no matter how well training goes). Instead you watch a small panel:

- **Mean reward.** The thing you want up. Track it on the training batches *and* on a fixed held-out set with greedy decoding; only the second tells you about real skill.
- **Reward std within groups, and `frac_zero_std`.** The fraction of groups with no spread. This is your "is there anything to learn" gauge. Near 1 means the task is too easy or too hard for the model right now.
- **Response length.** Mean completion tokens, and the fraction that hit the max length (truncated). Some growth is healthy on reasoning tasks. A curve racing to the cap is a disease.
- **KL to the reference.** How far you've wandered. Should rise slowly. A sudden spike means something broke out.
- **Entropy.** The average per-token <Term w="entropy">How spread out the model's next-token distribution is, in nats. High: many tokens plausible. Near zero: the model is certain, and every sample comes out the same.</Term>. It slowly falls as the model gets more confident. If it crashes to near zero, every sample in a group becomes identical, std becomes zero, and learning stops. That's collapse.
- **Clip fraction**, if you do more than one update per batch: the share of tokens whose ratio hit the clip.

### Where the time goes

One GRPO step has four phases: **generate** G completions for each prompt in the batch, **score** them, run the **reference** model forward to get its log-probs, then **train** the policy (forward and backward over prompt plus completion, then an optimizer step).

Scoring is nearly free with a Countdown verifier, and a few seconds per batch with a sandbox running unit tests. Training is one big parallel pass over every token at once: it's compute-bound and efficient. Generation is the slow one, because it writes one token at a time. From [Why ~17 tokens per second?](/lessons/bandwidth-bound/): every decode step reads every weight from memory, so it's bound by memory bandwidth, not compute. And completions in a batch finish at different lengths, so the batch waits for its longest answer.

```python
# countdown.py
import ast, random, re
from collections import Counter

def make_puzzle(rng: random.Random, n_nums: int = 3) -> dict:
    """Pick n numbers, combine them with random ops into a target, so a solution always exists."""
    while True:
        nums = [rng.randint(1, 20) for _ in range(n_nums)]
        expr = str(nums[0])
        for x in nums[1:]:
            expr = f"({expr} {rng.choice('+-*')} {x})"
        target = eval(expr)
        if 1 <= target <= 200:
            rng.shuffle(nums)
            return {"nums": nums, "target": target}

PROMPT = (
    "Using the numbers {nums}, write an arithmetic expression that equals {target}. "
    "Use each number exactly once. You may use + - * / and parentheses. "
    "Think briefly, then put only the expression inside <answer></answer>."
)

ANSWER_RE = re.compile(r"<answer>(.*?)</answer>", re.DOTALL)
ALLOWED = (ast.Expression, ast.BinOp, ast.Constant, ast.Add, ast.Sub,
           ast.Mult, ast.Div, ast.UnaryOp, ast.USub)

def safe_eval(expr: str):
    tree = ast.parse(expr, mode="eval")
    for node in ast.walk(tree):
        if not isinstance(node, ALLOWED):
            raise ValueError(f"disallowed: {type(node).__name__}")
        if isinstance(node, ast.Constant) and not isinstance(node.value, int):
            raise ValueError("only integer literals")
    return eval(compile(tree, "<answer>", "eval"), {"__builtins__": {}}, {})

def reward(completion: str, nums: list[int], target: int) -> float:
    """1.0 if the last <answer> uses exactly `nums` and evaluates to `target`, else 0.0."""
    found = ANSWER_RE.findall(completion)
    if not found:
        return 0.0
    expr = found[-1].strip()
    used = [int(t) for t in re.findall(r"\d+", expr)]
    if Counter(used) != Counter(nums):
        return 0.0
    try:
        value = safe_eval(expr)
    except Exception:  # syntax error, division by zero, disallowed node
        return 0.0
    return 1.0 if abs(value - target) < 1e-9 else 0.0

if __name__ == "__main__":
    assert reward("<answer>(3 + 7) * 4</answer>", [3, 7, 4], 40) == 1.0
    assert reward("<answer>40</answer>", [3, 7, 4], 40) == 0.0             # skipped the numbers
    assert reward("<answer>3 * 7 + 7 + 12</answer>", [3, 7, 4], 40) == 0.0  # wrong numbers
    assert reward("<answer>__import__('os')</answer>", [3, 7, 4], 40) == 0.0
    assert reward("<answer>(3 + 7) / 0 + 4</answer>", [3, 7, 4], 40) == 0.0
    assert reward("the answer is (3+7)*4", [3, 7, 4], 40) == 0.0             # no tags
    print("all verifier tests pass")
```

- Logits at position t predict the token at t+1. Slice `logits[:, prompt_len-1:-1]` against `input_ids[:, prompt_len:]`. Get this shift wrong and training will look plausible and learn nothing.
- Use `F.log_softmax(logits.float(), -1).gather(-1, ids.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)`, one micro-batch at a time, to avoid the 27 GB logits tensor. Entropy is `-(p * logp).sum(-1)` on the same micro-batch, under `torch.no_grad()`.
- `old_logp` and `ref_logp` are computed once, right after rollout, with `no_grad`. With one update per batch, `old_logp` equals `logp.detach()`, and you can use exactly that.
- Ratio: `torch.exp(logp - old_logp)`. Per-token KL: with `d = ref_logp - logp`, it's `torch.exp(d) - d - 1`.
- Advantage is per sequence; broadcast it with `adv[:, None]`.
- Reduce with the mask: per-sequence mean over tokens (`(x * mask).sum(-1) / mask.sum(-1)`), then mean over sequences. That's the paper's `1/|o_i|`. Break It has you try the alternative.
- Sanity test before training: with `logp == old_logp == ref_logp`, the loss value should be about `-adv.mean()`, which is ~0, and its gradient with respect to `logp` should be `-adv / (N · |o_i|)` on each unmasked token, where N is the number of sequences in the batch (the KL term's gradient is exactly zero there).

## Break It

**1. Remove the baseline.** Use `A = r` (raw 0/1 reward) instead of the group advantage. Every answer that passes gets pushed up and nothing ever gets pushed down. It still learns, more slowly and more noisily; with rewards in `[5, 6]` instead of `[0, 1]`, it barely learns at all. This is the 220x variance from Figure 2, live.

**2. Write a sloppy verifier.** Change `reward` to give 1.0 if the target number appears anywhere in the completion. Within a few dozen steps the model learns to write the target and stop. Reward goes to nearly 1, held-out accuracy (with the strict checker) doesn't move. That's reward hacking in its purest form, and it's why you keep the strict held-out check separate from the training reward. See [Rewards and verifiers](/lessons/verifiers-rewards/) for the subtler versions that happen with unit tests.

**3. Starve the signal.** Switch to 4-number puzzles (too hard) or 2-number ones (too easy) and watch `frac_zero_std` climb toward 1 and the reward curve flatten. Then raise G from 8 to 16 on the hard set and see how many groups come back to life. This is the same trade the interactive figure shows: more samples per prompt buy signal with generation time. DAPO (2025) went further and simply filtered out dead groups and resampled, so every batch has full signal.

**4. Let it run wild.** Set β = 0 and raise the learning rate 10-20x. Watch entropy and KL. Typical outcomes: entropy collapses, every sample in a group becomes the same text, std goes to 0, learning stops cold. Or the model finds one long template that sometimes passes and length runs to the cap. Then try the length bias directly: replace the per-sequence token mean (`1/|o_i|`) with a sum over tokens divided by a constant. Under the per-sequence mean, a long wrong answer is penalised less per token than a short wrong one, which nudges failing answers to get longer. Liu et al. (2025, "Dr. GRPO") argue this bias, together with the std division, explains part of the length growth reported in R1-style runs. Run both and see whether your length curves differ.

## Explain It Back

Answer out loud, as if in an interview. Then check.

<details>
<summary>1. Explain policy gradient to someone who knows supervised learning. Why does it need a baseline?</summary>

Supervised learning increases the log-probability of a target answer. Policy gradient samples answers from the model itself, scores them, and increases the log-probability of each sample in proportion to its score: it's supervised learning on your own samples, weighted by how good they were, and the weights can be negative. Mathematically it's an unbiased estimate of the gradient of expected reward, and it works even though the reward isn't differentiable, because you only differentiate the log-probs. The baseline: if all rewards are large and positive, every sample is pushed up and the useful signal is a small difference between large forces, so the estimate is very noisy. Subtracting the expected reward means only better-than-expected answers go up and worse ones go down. It doesn't change the gradient's average, only its variance; in a toy I ran, by about 220x.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What does PPO have that GRPO doesn't, and what does GRPO pay instead?</summary>

PPO uses a value model, a second network about the size of the policy, to predict the expected reward at every token; that's its baseline, and it must be trained alongside the policy. GRPO drops it and uses the mean reward of G samples of the same prompt as the baseline, so every token of an answer shares one advantage. Both use the clipped ratio objective and a KL penalty to a reference (PPO-RLHF usually puts KL in the reward, GRPO in the loss). GRPO saves the memory and the instability of training a critic, and pays in generation: G completions per prompt. That trade is good when rewards come at the end (a verifier's verdict), because a critic predicting a final 0/1 from partial text is hard to learn anyway.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Your GRPO reward curve went flat at 40%. Walk through what you'd check.</summary>

First `frac_zero_std` and which kind: if most groups are all-wrong, the remaining prompts are too hard for the model to ever sample a success; raise G, add easier prompts, or filter dead groups. If they're all-right, the data is exhausted; add harder prompts. Then read samples: is the model truncating at max length (no answer tag = 0)? Has entropy collapsed so all samples are identical? Then check the verifier on the samples by hand, for both false negatives (correct answers it rejects, like a format mismatch) and false positives. Then check held-out accuracy: if training reward is fine but held-out isn't, it's overfitting or hacking. Last, the boring bugs: the logit shift, the completion mask, the learning rate.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. How does the cost of one GRPO step scale with G, completion length, and model size? Where do you put money to go faster?</summary>

Generation cost scales roughly with (prompts × G) × completion length, and because decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, a larger batch of sequences is nearly free per token until you become compute-bound or run out of KV cache; KV cache memory grows with batch × length × layers, which caps the batch. Training cost scales with total tokens × model size (about 6 × params FLOPs per token) and is compute-bound and efficient. Long completions hurt twice: more decode steps, and the batch waits for its longest answer. Doubling model size roughly doubles both. Since generation usually dominates, money goes into inference: a real engine with continuous batching and paged KV cache, prefix caching (G samples share one prompt), and at larger scale separate GPUs for generation and training with weights synced between them. Fast inference is fast RL.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why did verifiable rewards make RL on LLMs practical, when RLHF with PPO already existed?</summary>

A learned reward model is a neural network with blind spots; optimise against it long enough and the policy exploits them (length, tone, flattery), so you have to stop early or keep the policy on a short leash. A verifier like "does the final answer match" or "do the hidden tests pass" is cheap, deterministic, and much harder to exploit, so you can train for far longer and the reward keeps meaning what you intended. Combined with GRPO dropping the critic, the whole recipe became simple enough to reproduce on a single machine. The limit: you can only apply it where correctness can be checked by code, and the model only improves on strategies it already sometimes finds.

</details>

- **Zhihong Shao et al., "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models" (2024)**, [arXiv:2402.03300](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300). Where GRPO is introduced. Section 4 has the objective and the outcome vs process supervision variants; the discussion after it frames SFT, rejection sampling, DPO, PPO and GRPO as one gradient formula with different weights.
- **DeepSeek-AI, "DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning" (2025)**, [arXiv:2501.12948](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948). R1-Zero's reward design, the response-length curve, and the honest list of what went wrong before they added cold-start data.
- **OpenAI Spinning Up, "Part 3: Intro to Policy Optimization"** by Joshua Achiam, [spinningup.openai.com](https://spinningup.openai.com/). The cleanest derivation of the policy gradient and why baselines don't add bias. Read it with a pen.
- **John Schulman et al., "Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms" (2017)**, [arXiv:1707.06347](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347). Short. Read for the clipped objective and Figure 1, which is the clip plot in this lesson.

- **Zichen Liu et al., "Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training: A Critical Perspective" (2025)**, [arXiv:2503.20783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783). The Dr. GRPO paper: the length and std-normalisation biases, and how much of "emergent long reasoning" they explain.
