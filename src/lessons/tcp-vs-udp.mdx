## The Problem

IP, from the last lesson, makes one promise: it will *try*. You hand it a packet with an address on it, and it will do its best to get it there. It might not arrive. It might arrive twice. Two packets sent in order might arrive swapped. Nobody tells you which.

Now look at what you actually do all day. `requests.post(...)` sends a 3 KB JSON body to the ranking service, and the service gets exactly those 3 KB, in order, with nothing missing. `scp` copies a 16 GB checkpoint to the Spark and the hash matches. A token stream from a model server arrives as `Hello`, ` world`, never ` worldHello`. Something between your code and IP is turning "probably, in some order" into "definitely, in order". That something is TCP, and it is running inside your kernel on every machine you own.

It isn't free. Every new connection costs a round trip before the first byte moves, which is 150 ms to Europe. A brand-new connection starts slowly on purpose and takes several more round trips to reach full speed. One lost packet stalls everything behind it, even data that already arrived. Small writes can sit in a buffer for 40 ms on purpose. And if you open and close connections fast enough, you can run your client out of port numbers.

Meanwhile some of the most latency-sensitive software in the world (DNS, video calls, games, WireGuard, Tailscale, and HTTP/3) deliberately uses UDP, the protocol that promises nothing. To understand why both exist, you're going to build the promise yourself, out of UDP, and feel each problem as it shows up.

<Origin year="1986" title="The internet's throughput falls by a factor of 1,000">

The first TCP specs (Cerf and Kahn's 1974 paper, then RFC 793 in 1981) already had sequence numbers, acks and retransmission. What they didn't have was any idea of *how fast* to send. A sender blasted out a full window of data, and if acks didn't come back in time, it sent everything again.

In October 1986, the link between Lawrence Berkeley Lab and UC Berkeley, 400 yards and three hops apart, dropped from 32 kilobits per second to 40 bits per second. The network wasn't broken. It was full of retransmissions: routers' queues overflowed, packets were dropped, senders timed out and resent everything, which overflowed the queues further. Almost every packet on the wire was a copy of one that had already been sent. This became known as congestion collapse.

Van Jacobson and Michael Karels worked out why and fixed it in the senders, without touching a single router. Their 1988 paper, "Congestion Avoidance and Control", added slow start, a much better retransmission timer, and the rule of backing off hard when you see loss. Those ideas are still in every TCP stack today. The internet works because every sender on it agrees to be polite.

</Origin>

## The Idea

### Ports: the apartment number

An IP address gets a packet to a machine. But your laptop is running a browser, Slack, an SSH session to the Spark, and a Python script talking to an API, all at once. When a packet arrives, which program gets it?

The answer is a <Term w="port">A 16-bit number (0 to 65535) that names one conversation endpoint on a machine. The IP address picks the building; the port picks the apartment.</Term>. Every TCP or UDP packet carries two: the source port and the destination port. A server program asks the kernel to <Term w="listen">Tell the kernel "deliver new connections for this port to me". Only one program can normally listen on a given port and address at a time; that's the "address already in use" error.</Term> on a port: `sshd` on 22, your FastAPI app on 8000. When your client connects out, the kernel picks a temporary <Term w="ephemeral port">A port the kernel picks for the client side of a connection, from a reserved range. macOS uses 49152-65535; Linux uses 32768-60999 by default.</Term> for its side.

Your program never touches ports or packets directly. It holds a <Term w="socket">The kernel object your program uses to send and receive network data. In code it's a file descriptor, a small integer, just like an open file. You read and write it; the kernel does the protocol.</Term>. `socket.socket()` in Python asks the kernel for one. Everything TCP does (the retries, the ordering, the timers) happens inside the kernel, on the far side of that socket. You see `send` and `recv`.

A TCP connection is named by four numbers: source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port. That <Term w="4-tuple">The (source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port) that uniquely identifies a TCP connection. Add the protocol and you get the 5-tuple that firewalls and load balancers hash on.</Term> is why one server port can hold thousands of connections at once: they all share the server half and differ in the client half.

### UDP: IP plus a return address

<Term w="UDP">User Datagram Protocol (RFC 768, 1980). Adds ports, a length and a checksum to an IP packet. No connection, no retries, no ordering. Each send is one packet.</Term> is almost nothing. It takes IP and adds exactly four fields, eight bytes in total: source port, destination port, length, and a <Term w="checksum">A small number computed from the packet's bytes. The receiver recomputes it; if it doesn't match, some bits got flipped on the way and the packet is thrown away.</Term>. That's the whole protocol.

What you get: each `sendto()` becomes one <Term w="datagram">A self-contained packet that carries its full destination address and is delivered (or not) on its own, with no relationship to the ones before or after.</Term> and each `recvfrom()` returns exactly one, whole, with its boundaries intact. What you don't get: any guarantee it arrives, arrives once, or arrives in order. If a router drops it, nobody resends it, and nobody tells you.

That sounds bad, but notice what it also means. There's no setup: the first packet carries data. There's no state on the server per client. And nothing ever waits: if packet 7 is lost, packet 8 is delivered the moment it shows up. For some jobs, those are exactly the properties you want.

### TCP: the promise

<Term w="TCP">Transmission Control Protocol. Gives two programs a reliable, ordered, two-way stream of bytes over unreliable IP, and slows down when the network is congested.</Term> promises something much bigger: whatever bytes you write into one end come out the other end, all of them, in the same order, exactly once, or you get an error. It looks like a pipe between two programs. It's called a <Term w="byte stream">A sequence of bytes with no built-in message boundaries. Two sends of 100 bytes might arrive as one recv of 200, or as recvs of 37 and 163. Framing messages is your job.</Term>, and that has a consequence people trip on for years: TCP doesn't know where your messages begin and end. `send(b"hello")` then `send(b"world")` might arrive as one `recv` returning `b"helloworld"`. Every protocol on top of TCP (HTTP, Postgres, Redis) has to say how long each message is.

Now let's build the promise, one trick at a time, the way you're about to in code.

### Trick 1: number everything, and ack it

Picture mailing a 300-page manuscript to a publisher, one page per envelope, through a postal service that loses some envelopes and delivers in random order. The first thing you'd do is number the pages. Then the publisher can put them back in order, notice gaps, and throw away duplicates.

That's a <Term w="sequence number">A counter stamped on each piece of data so the receiver can put pieces in order, spot gaps and drop duplicates. TCP counts bytes, not packets.</Term>. TCP numbers every byte of the stream; your channel will number packets, which is simpler and teaches the same thing.

Second, the publisher sends postcards back: "got everything up to page 41". That's an <Term w="ack">Acknowledgement. A message from the receiver saying what it has received. TCP's acks are cumulative: "I have every byte before N; send N next".</Term>. TCP's acks are <Term w="cumulative ack">An ack that says "I have everything before N", rather than listing pieces. One ack covers many packets, and a lost ack is repaired by any later one.</Term>, which is a lovely property: if one postcard is lost, the next one says the same thing and more.

### Trick 2: if you don't hear back, send it again

What if an envelope is lost? The publisher can't complain about a page they never knew existed. So you, the sender, start a timer when you post a page. If no postcard covering it comes back in time, you post it again. That's a <Term w="retransmission timeout">RTO. How long the sender waits for an ack before assuming the data was lost and resending it. Too short wastes bandwidth on copies; too long leaves the connection idle.</Term>.

Notice the sender can't tell the difference between "my page was lost" and "the postcard was lost" and "the postcard is just slow". In all three cases it resends. So the receiver must be ready for duplicates, which is why sequence numbers come first.

The simplest protocol you can build from these two tricks is <Term w="stop-and-wait">Send one packet, wait for its ack, then send the next. Correct, simple, and as slow as the round trip allows.</Term>: send page 1, wait for the postcard, send page 2. It works. It's also dreadfully slow, and you should be able to say exactly how slow before you build it.

### Trick 3: keep many in flight (the sliding window)

The fix is to post several pages before hearing back. Allow up to `W` unacknowledged packets in flight at once. When an ack arrives, the oldest ones are done, and you can post that many new ones. The set of "sent but not yet acked" numbers slides forward along the stream: a <Term w="sliding window">The range of sequence numbers a sender may have in flight unacknowledged. As acks arrive the window slides forward and new data can be sent.</Term>.

How big should `W` be? Big enough to keep the pipe full for a whole round trip. That's the <Term w="bandwidth-delay product">Bandwidth × round-trip time: how many bytes are "in the pipe" when it's full. A sender needs at least this much unacknowledged data in flight to use the link fully.</Term>, the same `bytes in flight = bandwidth × latency` from the latency lesson:

```text
1 Gb/s to Europe:  125 MB/s × 0.150 s ≈ 18.75 MB in flight
Home Wi-Fi to Spark over Tailscale:  ~60 MB/s × 0.005 s ≈ 300 KB
Datacenter: 12.5 GB/s (100 Gb/s) × 0.0002 s ≈ 2.5 MB
```

18.75 MB in flight, unacked, at all times, to fill one link to Europe. Remember that number; it explains several things later.

When a packet in the window is lost, what do you resend? The simplest answer is <Term w="go-back-N">On timeout, resend every unacked packet from the oldest one onward. The receiver only accepts the next expected packet and drops anything out of order. Simple, but wasteful under loss.</Term>: rewind to the oldest unacked packet and send everything from there again. It's wasteful (the receiver may already have most of those) but simple, and it's the version you'll build first. The smarter version, <Term w="selective repeat">Resend only the packets that were actually lost. The receiver buffers out-of-order packets and the sender needs to know which ones arrived. TCP does this with SACK.</Term>, resends only the missing ones.

Here is your build, as a picture. Start with window 1 and no loss, then raise the window until throughput stops growing, then add loss.

Things to try in the figure:

- **Window 9, no loss.** The diagonals pack together, the sender never idles, 100 KB/s.

- **Window 16, 5% loss.** Higher window, more retries, barely more goodput. Under loss, a bigger window mostly buys you bigger rewinds.

### What real TCP does better

Your channel and TCP share the skeleton. TCP adds a few refinements that each fix a weakness you'll see in your version:

**It measures the round trip and sets the timer from it.** Your timer will be a fixed number you pick. TCP times its own packets and keeps a smoothed RTT and a measure of how much it varies, then sets `RTO = smoothed RTT + 4 × variation` (Jacobson's 1988 formula, standardized in RFC 6298). After a timeout it doubles the RTO each time, so a dead link is probed less and less often. Linux won't go below 200 ms; the RFC says 1 second. That floor is why a lost packet on a 1 ms datacenter link can cost you 200 ms: a 200x stall.

**It doesn't wait for the timer when it can guess.** If packet 5 is lost but 6, 7, 8 arrive, the receiver acks "I want 5" three more times. Three of these <Term w="duplicate ack">An ack repeating the same "next byte I want", which means later data arrived but a gap is still open. Three in a row tell the sender a packet was almost certainly lost.</Term>s trigger <Term w="fast retransmit">Resending a packet after three duplicate acks instead of waiting for the timeout. Repairs a single loss in about one round trip.</Term>: resend 5 right now, about one round trip after the loss instead of a full timeout. Timeouts become the rare, expensive fallback.

**It resends only what's missing.** With <Term w="SACK">Selective acknowledgement (RFC 2018). An option in which the receiver lists the byte ranges it has beyond the gap, so the sender resends only the holes.</Term>, the receiver reports exactly which ranges it holds, so TCP is selective repeat, not go-back-N.

**It respects the receiver's space.** Each ack carries the window field from Figure 002: "I have this many bytes of buffer left". The sender never has more than that in flight. That's <Term w="flow control">Stopping a fast sender from overrunning a slow receiver. The receiver advertises how much buffer space it has; the sender stays within it.</Term>, and it protects the receiver. The field is 16 bits, so it maxes out at 64 KB, which at 150 ms caps you at 64 KB ÷ 0.15 s ≈ 430 KB/s no matter how fast the link. Long-pipe links forced the <Term w="window scaling">A TCP option (RFC 7323) agreed during the handshake that multiplies the 16-bit window by up to 2^14, allowing windows up to about 1 GB.</Term> option. You'll make the no-flow-control failure happen in Break It.

### Opening and closing: the handshake

Before any data moves, both sides need to agree on starting sequence numbers and confirm each can hear the other. TCP does it in three messages: the <Term w="three-way handshake">SYN ("here's my starting number"), SYN-ACK ("got yours, here's mine"), ACK ("got yours"). After it, both sides know the other is alive and which numbers to expect.</Term>.

Why three? Each side picks its own starting number and needs the other to confirm it. Client says "mine starts at x" (SYN). Server says "got x, mine starts at y" (SYN-ACK, two jobs in one packet). Client says "got y" (ACK). Two messages would confirm only one direction. The starting numbers are random, not zero, for two reasons: a stray packet from an old connection on the same 4-tuple shouldn't land inside the new one's numbers, and an attacker who can't see your traffic shouldn't be able to guess them and inject data.

### Keep-alive and connection reuse

Two different things share the name keep-alive, and mixing them up is common.

<Term w="HTTP keep-alive">Reusing one TCP connection for many HTTP requests instead of opening a new one each time. Default in HTTP/1.1. ### Congestion control: being polite

Flow control protects the receiver. Nothing so far protects the *network*. Between you and the server are routers with queues. If every sender sends as fast as its window allows, the queues fill, routers drop packets, senders time out and resend, the queues fill more. That's 1986.

So TCP keeps a second window, private to the sender: the <Term w="congestion window">cwnd. The sender's guess at how much data the network path can hold right now. The sender may have at most min(cwnd, receiver's window) bytes in flight.</Term>. Nobody tells the sender the right value. It has to discover it, and it uses loss as the signal that it went too far.

The classic algorithm (Reno, from Jacobson's work) has two phases:

1. <Term w="slow start">Start a new connection with a small cwnd and double it every round trip (add one packet per ack) until loss or a threshold. "Slow" compared to blasting the whole window at once; actually exponential.</Term>. Start with a small window (10 packets, about 14 KB, on modern stacks per RFC 6928) and double it every round trip. Exponential growth finds the right neighbourhood fast.
2. <Term w="AIMD">Additive increase, multiplicative decrease. Grow cwnd by one packet per round trip; on loss, cut it in half. Many senders doing this converge to sharing a link fairly.</Term>. Past a threshold, grow by just one packet per round trip. On loss, cut the window in half. On a timeout (a sign things are really bad), drop back to the start.

Plot the window over time and you get TCP's signature sawtooth.

### Nagle's algorithm, and why token streams turn it off

In the early 1980s, people typed into remote terminals over TCP. Each keystroke was one byte of data in a packet with 40 bytes of headers. On a busy, slow link, these <Term w="tinygram">A packet carrying a few bytes of data and 40+ bytes of headers. Many of them waste most of the link on headers.</Term>s clogged everything.

John Nagle's fix (RFC 896, 1984) is one rule: **if you have unacked data in flight, don't send a small packet. Buffer small writes until the ack comes back or you have a full packet's worth.** It is on by default in every TCP stack to this day.

Now meet its partner. Receivers often use <Term w="delayed ack">Instead of acking every packet at once, the receiver waits briefly (typically 40 ms on Linux, up to 200 ms on some systems) hoping to piggyback the ack on a reply or to ack two packets with one.</Term>s: they hold the ack for a bit, hoping to combine it with a reply. Put the two together and you get a famous stall. You write a small header, then a small body. The header goes out. The body is small and there's unacked data, so Nagle holds it. The receiver got a header but no full request, so it has nothing to reply with, so it delays its ack. Both sides wait until the delayed-ack timer fires, about 40 ms on Linux.

LLM token streaming is a sequence of exactly these small writes: one server-sent event of a few dozen bytes per token. With Nagle on, a token written while the previous one is unacked waits for that ack. Tokens arrive in clumps, and time-to-first-token and inter-token latency look worse than the model actually is. The fix is one socket option:

```python
sock.setsockopt(socket.IPPROTO_TCP, socket.TCP_NODELAY, 1)  # send small writes immediately
```

<Term w="TCP_NODELAY">A socket option that turns off Nagle's algorithm, so every write goes out as soon as the window allows. The right setting for interactive and streaming traffic.</Term> is the right setting for anything interactive: streaming, RPC, games over TCP, SSH. Many frameworks set it for you (Go's net package does by default), but don't assume: Python's plain `socket` does not (I checked: `getsockopt` returns 0 on a fresh socket on 3.14). Check yours with `getsockopt` rather than trusting a blog post.

### Head-of-line blocking

TCP's promise is "in order". Think about what that costs when one packet is lost. Packets 6, 7, 8, 9 have arrived and are sitting in the receiver's kernel. But 5 is missing, so the kernel can't give your program 6 through 9: that would break the order. Everything waits for 5's retransmission, a round trip at best and a full RTO at worst. That's <Term w="head-of-line blocking">When one lost or slow item at the front of an ordered queue holds up everything behind it, even items that are ready.</Term>.

For one file transfer, that's fine; you needed byte 5 before byte 6 anyway. It hurts when one TCP connection carries several *independent* things. HTTP/2 does exactly that: many requests multiplexed as streams over one connection. A lost packet carrying a piece of an image stalls the JSON response and the CSS too, even though their bytes are sitting right there in the kernel. On lossy networks (mobile, bad Wi-Fi), HTTP/2 over one TCP connection can do worse than HTTP/1.1's six separate connections.

### Why QUIC rebuilt TCP on top of UDP

By the 2010s, TCP's problems were clear: the handshake round trip plus a separate TLS handshake, head-of-line blocking across streams, and connections tied to a 4-tuple, so walking from Wi-Fi to cellular (new IP address) kills every connection. The obvious fix is a new transport protocol. The problem is that you can't deploy one.

TCP lives in the operating system kernel, so changing it means waiting years for every phone, laptop and server to update. Worse, the internet is full of <Term w="middlebox">Any device in the network path that inspects or rewrites packets beyond plain routing: NATs, firewalls, load balancers, corporate proxies. Many drop anything they don't recognise.</Term>es that only understand TCP and UDP and drop anything else. SCTP, a perfectly good transport with multiple streams standardized in 2000, never made it across the public internet for exactly this reason. Even new TCP options get stripped by middleboxes that "know" what TCP looks like. People call this <Term w="ossification">When a protocol can no longer change in practice, because so many devices in the path have hardcoded assumptions about what it looks like.</Term>.

So Google, and then the IETF, built <Term w="QUIC">A transport protocol (RFC 9000, 2021) that runs over UDP and provides TCP-like reliability and congestion control, multiple independent streams, built-in TLS 1.3, and connection IDs that survive IP changes. HTTP/3 runs on it.</Term> on top of UDP. UDP gets through middleboxes, and it is thin enough to be a blank canvas. Everything else (sequence numbers, acks, retransmission, congestion control: the same tricks you're about to write) lives inside the QUIC library, in user space, inside your browser or server, so it ships with an app update instead of a kernel update. And almost the whole QUIC header is encrypted, so middleboxes can't build assumptions about it and it can't ossify the same way.

What that buys:

- **One handshake, not two.** Transport and TLS 1.3 setup happen together in one round trip. Reconnecting to a server you've seen before can send data in the very first packet (<Term w="0-RTT">Sending application data in the first packet of a resumed connection, using keys from a previous session. Saves a round trip; the data can be replayed by an attacker, so it's only for safe requests.</Term>).
- **No head-of-line blocking across streams** (Figure 006, bottom).
- **Connection migration.** Connections are named by an ID, not the 4-tuple, so your phone changing networks keeps its connections.

<Term w="HTTP/3">HTTP over QUIC (RFC 9114, 2022). Same requests and responses as HTTP/2, new transport underneath.</Term> is HTTP on QUIC, and a large share of web traffic from big providers now uses it. The punchline for this lesson: when people needed a better TCP, they rebuilt reliability on UDP, exactly what you're about to do in Python, just with 30 more years of tricks.

### So when do you pick UDP?

Pick UDP when a late packet is as bad as a lost one, when a whole exchange fits in one packet, or when something above you already does reliability.

- **DNS.** One small question, one small answer, usually under 512 bytes. A TCP handshake would triple the cost. If the answer doesn't come back, the client just asks again. (DNS does switch to TCP for big answers.)
- **Games and live state.** Where a player was 100 ms ago is useless once you know where they are now. Retransmitting it would delay the fresh update behind the stale one, which is head-of-line blocking at its worst. Games send state over UDP and resend only what still matters.
- **Voice and video calls.** A video frame that arrives after it should have been shown is garbage. WebRTC and RTP use UDP and hide losses (skip, interpolate, ask for a fresh keyframe) instead of retransmitting.
- **Tunnels: WireGuard and Tailscale.** Your SSH connection to the Spark over Tailscale is TCP *inside* WireGuard packets that are UDP. If the tunnel were TCP, you'd have two reliability loops stacked: a loss would make both the outer and inner TCP back off and retransmit, and their timers fight. This is called TCP-over-TCP meltdown. A UDP tunnel just carries packets and lets the inner TCP handle loss, once. (When UDP is blocked entirely, Tailscale falls back to its DERP relays over HTTPS, and you'll feel it in the latency.)
- **QUIC itself**, for everything above.

<Task id="t0" title="Set up the packet format" agent="ok">

A shared module for the wire format, so the sender and receiver agree on bytes. Each packet is a 1-byte type, a 4-byte sequence number, then payload. `struct` packs them in network byte order (big-endian, the `!`).

```python
# wire.py
import struct

DATA, ACK, FIN = 0, 1, 2
HDR = struct.Struct("!BI")   # type (1 byte), seq (4 bytes, unsigned)
MSS = 1200                   # payload bytes per packet; keeps packets under typical MTUs

def pack(kind: int, seq: int, payload: bytes = b"") -> bytes:
    return HDR.pack(kind, seq) + payload

def unpack(b: bytes) -> tuple[int, int, bytes]:
    kind, seq = HDR.unpack_from(b)
    return kind, seq, b[HDR.size:]
```

Conventions for your protocol, so the pieces fit together:

- Sequence numbers count **packets**, starting at 0. The last packet of a file is a `FIN` whose `seq` is one past the last `DATA`.
- An `ACK` carries the **next sequence number the receiver wants** (cumulative), like TCP. `ACK 5` means "I have 0-4".
- Both sides bump their UDP receive buffer: `s.setsockopt(socket.SOL_SOCKET, socket.SO_RCVBUF, 4 << 20)`. You'll see why in Break It.

Two macOS-specific notes: a single UDP datagram can't be bigger than 9,216 bytes by default (`sysctl net.inet.udp.maxdgram`), so don't raise `MSS` past that; and a test file of random bytes is easy: `head -c 2000000 /dev/urandom > blob.bin`.

<Task id="t3" title="Sliding window (go-back-N)" agent="hands">

Same receiver (it already works for go-back-N: it only accepts `expected` and acks cumulatively). New sender:

```python
def send_window(data: bytes, addr, window: int, rto: float = 0.05) -> int:
    """State: base (oldest unacked), next (next new seq to send), one timer for `base`.
    Loop until base passes the FIN:
      - while next < base + window and there are packets left: send pkts[next], next += 1;
        if the timer isn't running, start it.
      - wait for an ACK until the timer's deadline.
        ACK a > base: base = a; restart the timer if anything is still unacked, else stop it.
      - timer expired: resend pkts[base:next] (go back N), restart the timer.
    Return retransmissions.
    """
```

Hints:

- Pre-build the whole list of packets once. Retransmissions resend the same bytes.
- `s.settimeout(max(0, deadline - time.monotonic()))` and catch `socket.timeout` (an alias of `TimeoutError`).
- Window 1 should behave identically to your stop-and-wait. That's your first test.
- Optional upgrade, worth doing: make the receiver buffer out-of-order packets (a dict `seq → payload`) and drain it when the gap fills. Your go-back-N sender still works, and fewer resends are wasted.

</Task>

What the numbers should tell you:

**Window 1 is the round trip, nothing else.** 1,200 bytes ÷ about 14 ms ≈ 0.085 MB/s. Your link (loopback) can do gigabytes per second; you're using a ten-thousandth of it. Same lesson as the stop-and-wait Predict, at a smaller scale.

**At 0% loss, throughput scales linearly with the window.** 8x the window, 8x the speed, all the way to 64. You never reached the bandwidth-delay product: loopback is so fast that the "pipe" at 12 ms holds far more than 64 packets. The window is the only limit, which is also why a TCP connection with a too-small window or buffer is slow on a fast long link.

**Loss hurts big windows more than small ones.** At 5% loss, window 1 kept 60% of its speed; window 64 kept 19%. Every timeout idles a window-64 sender for 50 ms (three or four round trips) *and* then resends up to 64 packets. Look at the retransmission count: at 5% loss and window 64, there were 1,408 resends for 1,668 packets of data. Most of those were packets the receiver already had. That's go-back-N's waste, and why TCP uses SACK and fast retransmit.

**Against TCP.** Use the Mathis formula to predict the TCP column before you measure on the Spark. With 1,448-byte packets, a 20 ms netem round trip and 1% loss: `1448 / 0.020 × 1.22 / 0.1 ≈ 0.9 MB/s`; at 5%, about 0.4 MB/s. Treat these as rough: modern stacks with SACK and CUBIC do somewhat better than the formula. You may well find your window-64 channel beats TCP under random loss. That's not because you're cleverer than TCP. It's because yours never slows down when it sees loss. TCP assumes loss means congestion and backs off to protect everyone else on the link. Your protocol, on a shared network, would be the 1986 sender.

## Break It

**1. Set the timer shorter than the round trip.** Run with `rto=0.005` (5 ms against a 12 ms round trip) at zero loss. On my Mac, a 1 MB transfer at window 8 still worked (hash matched, about the same speed) but sent **1,582 retransmissions for 834 packets**: nearly every packet went twice, because the timer fired before any ack could possibly have come back. Throughput barely changed, so you might never notice, but you doubled your load on the network. Now picture every sender on a congested link doing that. This is why TCP measures the RTT continuously instead of trusting a constant, and why it backs the timer off exponentially after each timeout.

**2. Run two transfers against one receiver.** Make your receiver loop forever, serving one transfer after another without resetting cleanly, and run the sender twice in a row quickly. I did this by accident while writing this lesson: the receiver, still re-acking the end of transfer 1, sent the old file's final ACK number; the new sender read that as "everything up to there received", skipped most of its data, and reported a nonsense 70 MB/s while the receiver got garbage. Old packets with numbers that look valid landed in a new conversation. That is exactly what TCP's random starting sequence numbers and TIME_WAIT exist to prevent. Fix it the way TCP does: add a random connection ID (or random starting sequence number) to every packet and have each side ignore packets that don't match.

**3. Remove flow control.** Shrink the receiver's buffer to 64 KB (`SO_RCVBUF`) and make it slow (a small busy loop per packet), then send directly to it with no proxy, so there is no loss at all except what the receiver causes. On my Mac, a 10 MB transfer at window 16 ran at **58.6 MB/s with zero retransmissions**. At window 256: **1.8 MB/s and 25,411 retransmissions**. The receiver's kernel buffer overflowed and silently dropped datagrams, and your sender treated it as network loss. A bigger window made it 30x slower. Real TCP avoids this because each ack advertises the receiver's free space. Add a `window` field to your ACK packets and make the sender respect it.

**4. Cause the Nagle stall.** With plain TCP, write a client that sends a 10-byte "header", then a 10-byte "body", then waits for a 2-byte reply; the server replies only after it has all 20 bytes. Time 50 requests, then add `TCP_NODELAY` on the client and time again. On the Spark (Linux) you should see something like 40 ms per request with Nagle and well under a millisecond without; that's the delayed-ack timer. On my Mac's loopback I could *not* reproduce it (0.03 ms either way; macOS's delayed-ack behaviour on loopback differs), so treat the Linux number as check-it-yourself. Then fix it the other good way: build the whole message and send it in one `sendall`.

## Explain It Back

Answer out loud, in plain words, as if in an interview. Then check.

<details>
<summary>1. What does TCP give you that UDP doesn't, and how does it do it?</summary>

UDP gives you ports and a checksum on top of IP: one packet per send, which may be lost, duplicated or reordered. TCP gives a reliable, ordered byte stream. It numbers every byte (sequence numbers), the receiver acks what it has cumulatively, the sender keeps a timer and resends anything not acked in time (or sooner, after three duplicate acks), it keeps many packets in flight with a sliding window, it never sends more than the receiver says it has room for (flow control), and it adjusts its sending rate to avoid overloading the network (congestion control: slow start, then additive increase, halve on loss). The cost is a handshake before data, per-connection state, and head-of-line blocking.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why is stop-and-wait slow, and how big a window do you need?</summary>

Because you send one packet per round trip, throughput is packet size ÷ RTT, regardless of link speed: 1,200 bytes every 150 ms is 8 KB/s even on a 10 Gb/s link. To fill the link you need a full round trip's worth of data in flight: the bandwidth-delay product. 1 Gb/s × 150 ms is about 18.75 MB. That's why TCP needed window scaling (the original 16-bit window caps at 64 KB) and why socket buffer sizes matter on long, fast links.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. An API client makes 1,000 small requests to a service in another region, opening a new connection each time. What does that cost, and what do you change?</summary>

Each new HTTPS connection pays a TCP handshake (1 RTT) and a TLS handshake (1 more RTT with TLS 1.3) before the request's own round trip, and starts in slow start. At a 70 ms RTT that's about 210 ms per request instead of 70 ms, so 3x slower, before counting server work. Each closed connection also leaves a TIME_WAIT entry on the closing side; at high rates to one destination you can exhaust ephemeral ports (about 28k on Linux, held 60 s each, so roughly 470 new connections/s). Fix: a connection pool (`requests.Session`, `httpx.Client`) so connections stay open, plus running requests concurrently if they're independent. If the connections sit idle through a NAT or load balancer, add keepalives or expect to reconnect.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. How does TCP's throughput scale with distance and loss? Why might one flow get 1 MB/s on a 1 Gb/s link?</summary>

TCP's rate is about window ÷ RTT, and with loss-based congestion control the window is set by how often packets are lost. The Mathis estimate is (MSS ÷ RTT) × 1.22 ÷ √loss: double the RTT, halve the speed; four times the loss, half the speed. At 150 ms and 1% loss that's around 120 KB/s whatever the link speed. Short transfers are worse: they never leave slow start, which needs about 7 round trips to deliver 1 MB from a 10-packet initial window. Fixes: shorten the RTT (serve from closer), reuse warm connections, use several connections in parallel, or use a congestion controller like BBR that doesn't treat random loss as congestion.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why is HTTP/3 built on UDP instead of fixing TCP? And why does Tailscale use UDP?</summary>

TCP lives in OS kernels and its shape is assumed by middleboxes (NATs, firewalls) all over the internet, so changing it takes a decade and new transport protocols get dropped. UDP passes through, and is thin enough to build on. QUIC puts reliability, congestion control and TLS 1.3 in a user-space library over UDP: one combined handshake, independent streams so one loss doesn't stall everything (no cross-stream head-of-line blocking), connection IDs that survive network changes, and an encrypted header that can't ossify. Tailscale/WireGuard tunnel your TCP traffic; if the tunnel were also TCP, a loss would trigger two stacked retransmission and backoff loops that interfere (TCP-over-TCP meltdown). A UDP tunnel delivers packets or drops them, and lets the inner TCP recover once.

</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Your token stream looks bursty: tokens arrive in clumps every ~40 ms. What's your first guess?</summary>

Nagle's algorithm interacting with delayed acks. The server writes one small SSE event per token; with Nagle on, a small write is held while earlier data is unacked, and the client delays its ack hoping to piggyback it, so writes go out in batches when the delayed-ack timer fires. Check `TCP_NODELAY` on the server's socket (and any proxy in between, like nginx or a load balancer) and turn it on. Also check for buffering at other layers: the web framework, a reverse proxy with response buffering on, or gzip on the stream.

</details>

## Go Deeper

- **Van Jacobson and Michael J. Karels, "Congestion Avoidance and Control"**, SIGCOMM 1988. The paper behind the Origin box: slow start, the RTT estimator and AIMD, explained by the people who fixed the 1986 collapse. Short, readable, and the appendix has the actual timer code.
- **James F. Kurose and Keith W. Ross, _Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach_, Chapter 3 (Transport Layer)**. Builds reliable data transfer step by step (rdt 1.0 to 3.0, then go-back-N and selective repeat) with the same structure as your build. The best textbook treatment of what you just wrote.
- **Ilya Grigorik, _High Performance Browser Networking_**, free at [hpbn.co](https://hpbn.co/). Chapters "Building Blocks of TCP" and "Building Blocks of UDP" cover handshake cost, slow start, head-of-line blocking and bandwidth-delay product with a latency-first, practical eye.
- **W. Richard Stevens, _TCP/IP Illustrated, Volume 1_** (2nd edition revised by Kevin Fall). Every TCP behaviour shown as real tcpdump traces. The reference to keep next to Wireshark.
- **Neal Cardwell, Yuchung Cheng, C. Stephen Gunn, Soheil Hassas Yeganeh, Van Jacobson, "BBR: Congestion-Based Congestion Control"**, ACM Queue, 2016. Why loss is the wrong signal on modern networks, and what to measure instead.
- **Adam Langley et al., "The QUIC Transport Protocol: Design and Internet-Scale Deployment"**, SIGCOMM 2017. Google's account of why they built QUIC over UDP, what middleboxes did to them, and what it bought in real latency.
- **RFC 9293 (TCP, 2022), RFC 768 (UDP, 1980), RFC 896 (Nagle, 1984), RFC 9000 (QUIC, 2021)**, at [rfc-editor.org](https://www.rfc-editor.org/). RFC 768 is three pages; read it in full just to see how little UDP is. RFC 896 is short and a lovely period piece.
- **David Anderson, "How NAT traversal works"**, Tailscale blog, 2020. How Tailscale gets UDP through NATs and firewalls, and when it gives up and relays. The perfect bridge to [the home server lesson](/lessons/ssh-and-tailscale/).
