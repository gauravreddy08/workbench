An LLM judge is a language model you hand someone else's answer to, along with grading instructions, and ask for a mark. It's the cheapest way to grade thousands of answers that no simple program can check, like "is this search result actually what the person wanted?" It never gets tired. It also has habits that have nothing to do with quality, and it won't tell you about them.

That matters the moment a judge's verdict decides something. If you use one to decide whether the new cross-encoder beats the old one, its habits become your conclusion. If you use one to grade an agent's runs every night, its blind spots become the agent's blind spots. And if you ever train a model to please a judge, the model will learn those habits on purpose.

This builds on [Why is the eval separate?](/lessons/why-evals-are-separate/), which explains why the grader shouldn't share the model's blind spots, and on [Is that improvement real?](/lessons/statistics-for-evals/), which gives you error bars for any score, including a judge's. It leads to [Ranking metrics and eval sets](/lessons/ranking-metrics/), where a trusted judge can label results at scale, and to [Rewards and verifiers](/lessons/verifiers-rewards/), where grading becomes the signal a model trains on.

## Today

- Explain why people grade model outputs with another model, and the three ways to ask it: score one answer, compare two, or check against a known-good answer.
- Name the judge's known habits (favouring whichever answer is shown first, favouring longer answers, favouring its own writing, trusting a confident tone, rewarding tidy formatting) and the specific fix for each, including which fixes do *not* work for which habit.
- Treat a judge as what it is, a sorter that puts each item in a bucket, and measure it like one: how often it agrees with your own labels, how much of that agreement beats lucky guessing, which mistakes it makes, and whether its stated confidence means anything.
- Write grading instructions that a model can apply consistently, for search-result relevance and for "did the agent finish the task".
- Work out what a judging run costs before you start it, and when you should not use a judge at all.

Read [Why is the eval separate?](/lessons/why-evals-are-separate/) first, and ideally [Is that improvement real?](/lessons/statistics-for-evals/) too. Budget one evening to read, one to label 100 pairs yourself, and one to build and improve the judge, for a few dollars of API spend at most.

## The Problem

You have retrained the cross-encoder. You want to know if the new ranker is better than the old one, and you don't have relevance labels for this week's queries. So you do what everyone does. You take 500 queries, get the top result from each ranker, and ask a strong model: "Here is a query and two search results. Which is more relevant? Answer A or B."

The new ranker wins 64% of the time. Ship it?

Then, out of curiosity, you rerun the exact same 500 comparisons with the two results swapped, so the old ranker's result is shown first. Now the *old* ranker wins 58% of the time. Nothing about the results changed. Only the order on the page did.

Then you notice the new ranker tends to surface longer pages with longer snippets. You trim every snippet to 40 words and rerun. The new ranker's win rate drops to 51%.

So what did the judge measure? Some mix of relevance, which slot the answer sat in, and how many words it had. You can't tell how much of each, because you never checked the judge against anything. You used it as a ruler without asking whether the ruler was straight.

That is this lesson. An LLM judge is genuinely useful: it's cheap, it never gets tired, and it can grade things no regex can. But it's a measuring instrument, and instruments have to be calibrated against a known reference before you trust a reading. The reference here is you, with a hundred careful labels.

Every judge you build today inherits those failure modes, and the paper's fixes (swap the order, give it a reference answer, make it reason first) are still the starting point.

## The Idea

### Why a model, and not a script or a person

You have three ways to grade an output.

1. **A program.** `assert extracted_price == 19.99`. Exact, free, instant. Works only when "correct" can be written as code.
2. **A person.** Handles anything. Costs minutes and dollars per item, gets tired, disagrees with other people, and doesn't scale to 10,000 items every night.
3. **A model.** Handles most open-ended things a person can, at a few tenths of a cent per item, in seconds, at any volume. But it's a model, so it has blind spots and biases, and you don't know what they are until you measure.

The judge earns its place in the gap between 1 and 2: outputs too open-ended for code ("is this summary faithful?", "is this result relevant to the query?", "did the agent's final answer actually address the user's request?") and too numerous for people. Its job is to stand in for a person. So the only question that matters about any judge is: **how often does it say what a careful person would have said?** Everything else in this lesson is a way of answering that question or of making the answer better.

**Pointwise.** Show one answer, get a score on a fixed scale, like "relevance 0 to 3" or "helpfulness 1 to 5". It's the natural fit when you want an absolute number you can track over time, or when you only have one system's outputs. Its weakness is that the scale is fuzzy: what's the difference between a 3 and a 4? Two runs of the same judge, or the same judge on different days of prompt tweaks, can drift. Models also tend to bunch their scores (lots of 4s, few 1s), which makes small differences invisible. The fix is a <Term w="rubric">The written grading instructions: what to look for, and what each score means. A good rubric describes each score level with a concrete example, so two graders would give the same score.</Term> where every level is pinned to a concrete description, an <Term w="anchor" label="anchor (rubric)">A concrete example or description attached to one score level, like "2 = the page is about the right topic but does not answer the question". Anchors stop the scale from drifting.</Term>.

**Pairwise.** Show two answers, ask which is better. This is easier, for models and for people, for the same reason it's easier to say which of two coffees is stronger than to rate one coffee's strength out of 10. Relative judgements are more stable than absolute ones. Pairwise is what Chatbot Arena uses, and it's the natural fit for your actual question, "is ranker B better than ranker A?". Its weakness is the one in the opening scene: the two answers have to be shown in some order, and the order leaks into the verdict. It also scales badly if you want to rank many systems (every pair is a comparison), and a pairwise win rate tells you "better" but not "good enough".

**Reference-guided.** Give the judge a known-correct answer and ask whether the candidate matches it in substance. This turns "is this right?" (hard; the judge has to know the answer) into "does this say the same thing as that?" (much easier). Zheng et al. found it mattered most for maths and reasoning, where the judge would otherwise be fooled by a confident wrong solution it couldn't check. It needs a gold answer per item, which you often have for extraction and QA tasks and rarely have for open-ended ones.

You can mix them. A common pattern for agents: reference-guided where the task has a known answer, pointwise with a strict rubric where it doesn't.

### The biases, and where they come from

A judge is a language model reading a prompt. Everything that nudges a language model's next token nudges the verdict, including things that have nothing to do with quality. Here are the ones that have been measured, roughly in order of how much they'll hurt you.

**Position bias.** The judge prefers one slot, usually the first, regardless of content. Zheng et al. measured it by asking each question twice with the order swapped and counting how often the verdict stayed the same. GPT-4 was consistent about 65% of the time in their setup; weaker judges were far worse (one was consistent under a quarter of the time and almost always picked the first answer). A separate 2023 paper, Wang et al.'s "Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators", showed that just swapping the order could flip which of two models "won" a benchmark. Where does it come from? Partly from training data (in text, the first option listed is often the favoured one), partly from attention: the first answer is read with nothing to compare it to, the second is read in light of the first.

**Length (verbosity) bias.** Longer answers win more often, even when the extra length adds nothing. Zheng et al. tested this with a "repetitive list" attack: they took answers containing lists and padded them by rephrasing items already there, adding no new information. Weaker judges preferred the padded version over 90% of the time; GPT-4 was fooled much less often, but not never. This one is subtle because length is *sometimes* a real signal: a longer answer often is more complete. The judge learned a correlation that's real on average and wrong exactly when someone is padding. This matters enormously when the thing you're judging is being *trained against* the judge. A model optimised to win judge comparisons learns to be long. AlpacaEval, a popular judge-based leaderboard, ended up publishing a "length-controlled" version in 2024 for exactly this reason.

**Self-preference.** A judge tends to rate outputs from its own model family higher. Zheng et al. saw signs of it; Panickssery et al. (2024) showed that models can recognise their own outputs better than chance and that this recognition correlates with favouring them. The plain explanation: a model's own outputs are, by definition, the text it finds most likely, and "this reads naturally to me" leaks into "this is good". If you judge your fine-tuned Llama against GPT outputs using a GPT judge, the judge has a stake in the race.

**Confident tone.** Answers that sound sure of themselves get rated higher than hedged ones with the same content, and fluent wrong answers can beat halting right ones. Hosking et al. (2023) found that assertiveness skews *human* ratings of factuality too, so this isn't only a model problem, but a judge has no way to check a fact it doesn't know. This is the cousin of <Term w="sycophancy">A model's tendency to tell people what they seem to want to hear, or to agree with whoever sounds most certain, instead of what's true.</Term>: the judge defers to whichever answer sounds most like an authority.

**Format bias.** Markdown headers, bullet points, bold text, and citations-shaped brackets all look like effort. Judges reward them. For search snippets this shows up as preferring results with structured, list-like text over plain prose that answers the query better.

Notice the pattern. Every bias is the judge using a feature that *correlates* with quality (position in a list, length, fluency, familiar style, formatting) instead of quality itself. You'd do the same if you had to grade 10,000 essays in an afternoon. The fix in each case is to break the correlation: either remove the feature from what the judge sees, or measure the judge's response to the feature and cancel it out.

Swapping cancels **position** bias, and does nothing for **length** bias.

Position bias is a push toward a *slot*. When you swap the order, the push lands on the other answer. Ask both ways and the two pushes point in opposite directions, so averaging cancels them.

Length bias is a push toward a *property of the answer*. The longer answer is longer in both orders, so the push points at the same answer both times. Averaging two verdicts that are both tilted the same way gives you a verdict that's still tilted. Two numbers fall out of this for free, and you should report both for any pairwise judge:

- **Consistency rate**: the fraction of pairs where the same answer wins in both orders. A perfect, unbiased judge on clear-cut pairs would be near 100%. On genuinely close pairs it'll be lower even without bias, because the judge is a bit noisy.
- **First-slot win rate**: across all 2N calls, how often did the first-shown answer win? If the answers were randomly assigned to slots, this should be close to 50%. 60% means a real tilt. 80% means the judge is mostly reading the position.

There are two ways to combine the two verdicts. The **hard** way: if they agree, that's the answer; if they disagree, call it a tie. The **soft** way: get the judge's probability that the first answer wins (from token <Term w="logprobs">Log-probabilities. Many APIs can return how likely the model thought each possible next token was. The probability it gave to "A" versus "B" is a free confidence score.</Term> if your API returns them, or by sampling several times), convert both to "probability A wins", and average.

The soft version is better than it sounds. Model the judge's internal score for "first wins" as *quality gap + position push + noise*. In order 1 that's `gap + push`; in order 2, flipped back to A's point of view, it's `gap − push`. Averaging the two probabilities lands above 50% exactly when the sum of those two scores is positive, and in the sum the push cancels. So with a judge that gives probabilities, averaging across both orders removes a constant position bias completely, not approximately. The hard version can't do this: it throws away the "how sure" and can only shrug with a tie.

Things to try, and what they should show:

1. **Both sliders at zero.** One order gets about 87% right, the hard swap about the same, and the averaged swap about 95%. Averaging two independent looks at the same pair also averages away some of the noise. That's a bonus on top of bias removal.
2. **Position bias up to 1.0.** One-order accuracy drops to about 72%, the first slot wins 78% of calls, consistency drops to 43%. The averaged verdict stays at 95%. The hard swap stays down with one order, because every inconsistent pair becomes a tie worth half a point.
3. **Position bias at 2.0 or more.** The judge now always picks the first slot. One order is a coin flip, consistency is near zero, and the averaged verdict is *still* 95%. That's the cancellation, working even when the bias is overwhelming. (In real life, a judge this biased has probably stopped reading carefully at all, so the "fixed noise" assumption would break. Treat this end of the slider as a thought experiment.)
4. **Position back to zero, length bias at 1.0, pad the losers on.** Every pair now has the worse answer as the longer one. All three methods drop to around 65%, and the swap gives you no help. At length bias 2.0, all three fall below chance: the judge is now reliably picking the padded loser.

The lesson in one line: **swapping fixes biases tied to the slot; it can't fix biases tied to the answer.** For those you need something else.

### Mitigations, and which bias each one hits

| Mitigation | What it does | Hits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Swap order and average | Ask both ways; average probabilities, or call disagreement a tie | Position |
| Length control | Trim or cap answer length before judging; or regress verdicts on the length difference and report the length-adjusted win rate (what AlpacaEval's length-controlled version does) | Length |
| Rubric with concrete anchors | Define each verdict with examples, including "longer is not better" and "formatting does not count" said explicitly | Length, format, scale drift |
| Reasoning before verdict | Make the judge write its analysis first, then the verdict on the last line | Snap judgements, position (somewhat) |
| Reference answer | Give the judge a known-correct answer to compare against | Confident-but-wrong, maths |
| Different model family | Judge with a model from a different family than the ones being compared | Self-preference |
| Several judges | Majority vote across 3 different judge models, or across 3 samples of one | Noise, one model's quirks |
| Hide the source | Never tell the judge which system produced which answer | Brand or name bias |

Two of these deserve a closer look.

**Reasoning before verdict.** If the prompt asks for the verdict first and then an explanation, the model commits to a letter in its first output token and then writes a justification for whatever it already said. That's a <Term w="post-hoc rationalisation">Explaining a decision after it was already made. The explanation sounds like reasoning but had no influence on the decision.</Term>, and it's worthless as a check. Ask for the analysis first, then the verdict on its own final line. Now the verdict token is generated *after*, and conditioned on, the reasoning. This is the same chain-of-thought effect you know from reasoning tasks. It costs output tokens, which, as you'll see in the cost section, are the expensive ones. (If your judge model thinks internally before answering, some of this happens for free, but you still pay for those thinking tokens as output.)

**Different model family.** This is [Why is the eval separate?](/lessons/why-evals-are-separate/) applied to judges. A judge that shares training data, tokenizer and style with one of the contestants shares its blind spots. If you're comparing two of your own fine-tunes of the same base, this matters less (neither is "the judge's own"). If you're comparing your model against a vendor model, use a judge from a third family, or use two judges from different families and only trust verdicts they agree on.

### The judge is a classifier

Here's the reframe that makes everything else fall into place. A pairwise judge takes an input (query, A, B) and outputs a label (A, B, or tie). That's a classifier. Your hand labels are the ground truth. You know exactly how to evaluate a classifier: you build a confusion matrix and compute agreement metrics. People skip this for judges because the judge *feels* like an oracle. It isn't. It's a 3-class classifier with unknown accuracy, and it's about to grade the model you'll ship.

**Accuracy** (raw agreement) is the diagonal over the total: 74/100. Easy to understand, and easy to fool. Suppose 90% of your pairs are ones where the new ranker is clearly better. A judge that always answers "A" scores 90% accuracy and has learned nothing.

**Cohen's kappa** fixes that by asking how much better than luck the judge does. Write <Term w="p_o">Observed agreement: the fraction of items where the judge and you gave the same label. Just accuracy.</Term> for the observed agreement and <Term w="p_e">Expected agreement by chance: how often you'd match if the judge kept its overall label mix (say 49% A, 41% B, 10% tie) but assigned labels at random. Computed from the row and column totals.</Term> for the agreement you'd expect by chance given how often each of you uses each label. Then:

```text
kappa = (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e)
```

Read it as "of the room above luck, how much did the judge fill?". Here p_e is 0.45×0.49 + 0.40×0.41 + 0.15×0.10 ≈ 0.40, so kappa = (0.74 − 0.40) / 0.60 ≈ 0.57. The "always A" judge on the 90/10 set has p_o = 0.9 and p_e = 0.9, so kappa = 0. (I checked both with a few lines of Python; you'll write the same function in Build.) Kappa is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 for luck-level, negative for worse than luck. A common rough reading, from Landis and Koch in 1977, calls 0.41-0.60 "moderate" and 0.61-0.80 "substantial". Don't treat those as law. The useful comparison is with **human-human kappa on the same items**: if you and a colleague only agree at 0.65, a judge at 0.6 is about as good as a second person, and you can't expect 0.9.

**The confusion matrix** tells you *how* it's wrong, which accuracy and kappa can't. In Figure 004 the judge almost never says tie: of the 15 pairs you called a tie, it found 4. That's a specific, fixable rubric problem ("say tie when neither result answers the query better; do not break ties on length"), and you'd never see it in a single number.

**For pointwise scores** (say 0-3 relevance), use rank correlation between the judge's scores and yours. <Term w="Spearman correlation">Correlation between two lists after replacing each value with its rank. 1 means the two orderings agree perfectly, 0 means no relation. It only cares about order, not the exact numbers, so a judge that's consistently one point harsh still scores well.</Term> asks "does the judge put items in the same order as you?", which is usually what you need for comparing systems. If you also need the absolute numbers right (because you report "average relevance 2.3"), check exact-match agreement and a weighted kappa as well.

**Calibration.** If your judge gives a probability or a confidence, check whether it means anything. Bucket the judge's verdicts by its stated confidence (say 50-60%, 60-70%, ..., 90-100%) and check accuracy in each bucket against your labels. A <Term w="calibrated">A model is calibrated if, among all the times it says "80% sure", it's right about 80% of the time.</Term> judge's 90% bucket is right about 90% of the time. This matters because calibration is what lets you *act* on confidence: auto-accept verdicts above 90%, send the rest to a human. If the 90% bucket is only right 70% of the time, that routing rule is silently wrong.

**Confidence intervals.** 100 labels is not a lot. A judge with 80% agreement on 100 items has a 95% interval of roughly ±8 points (by the normal approximation, `1.96 × sqrt(0.8 × 0.2 / 100)` ≈ 0.078). So "rubric v2 got 82%, v1 got 78%" is not, on its own, evidence that v2 is better. [Is that improvement real?](/lessons/statistics-for-evals/) covers the bootstrap and the paired test that answer it properly. Use the paired version here: both rubrics graded the *same* 100 pairs, so compare them pair by pair.

### Writing rubrics that hold

A rubric is a specification, and the judge is a very literal, very fast, slightly lazy reader. The same rules that make a spec good for a contractor make it good for a judge: say what you mean, define the edge cases, give examples.

**Search-result relevance.** Your domain. The standard way information retrieval people do this is a graded scale with written definitions, and TREC-style judging (and Bing's large-scale LLM relevance labelling, Thomas et al. 2023) uses something like this:

```text
You are judging whether a web page is a good result for a search query.
Judge only relevance to what the searcher wanted. Ignore length, writing quality, and formatting.

3 = Answers it. A searcher would stop here: the page directly gives what the query asks for.
    e.g. query "python sort dict by value", page shows sorted(d.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1]).
2 = Useful. On the right topic and contains part of the answer, or leads to it in one click.
    e.g. same query, page explains sorting lists of tuples, without dicts.
1 = Related. Same general topic, but a searcher would not find the answer here.
    e.g. a page about dict comprehension syntax.
0 = Not relevant, spam, or a broken page.

For a PAIR, choose the result with the higher grade. If both have the same grade, answer TIE.
Do not break ties on length or detail.
```

The things that make this work: each level is a *behaviour of the searcher* ("would stop here"), not an adjective ("highly relevant"); each level has an example from the domain; the irrelevant features are named and excluded; and the tie rule is explicit. That last line directly attacks the tie blindness in Figure 004.

**Agent task success.** For agents (TinyFish's world), "did it work?" is usually a mix of things a program can check and things it can't. Split them.

```text
Task: {task}
Final answer from the agent: {answer}
Final page URL: {url}
Trajectory summary: {steps}

Answer each question YES or NO, with one sentence of evidence quoted from the inputs:
1. Did the agent reach a page where the requested information is visible?
2. Does the final answer contain the specific information requested (not a description of where to find it)?
3. Is every value in the final answer supported by text on the final page? Quote it.
4. Did the agent do anything the task did not ask for that changes state (submitting forms, buying, logging in)?

SUCCESS only if 1, 2 and 3 are YES and 4 is NO. Otherwise FAIL.
```

Two ideas here. First, **decompose**: four narrow yes/no questions are each much easier to judge reliably than one fuzzy "did it succeed (1-10)?", and when it fails you know *which* part failed. Second, **demand evidence**: "quote the text" forces the judge to ground its answer in the input instead of in how confident the agent sounded, which is your defence against the confident-tone bias.

**General rules for any rubric:**

- Binary or 3-way questions beat 1-10 scales. If you can't say what a 6 means versus a 7, neither can the judge.
- Name the features to ignore: length, formatting, tone, which answer came first.
- Put the verdict on the last line in a fixed format (`VERDICT: A`), and parse it strictly. If it doesn't parse, that's a failure you count, not a silent default.
- Iterate on the rubric using a dev split of your labels and report the final number on a held-out split. Otherwise you're tuning the rubric to your 100 examples, which is overfitting by a different name.

### What it costs

Judging is cheap, but "cheap times a lot" is how you get surprise bills. Do the arithmetic before every run:

```text
cost = items × calls per item × (input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price)
```

A judge prompt has two parts. The **rubric** (system prompt, scale definitions, maybe a few worked examples) is identical for every item and can easily be 1,000-2,000 tokens. The **item** (query plus two results) changes every call, maybe 300-500 tokens. Then the judge writes its reasoning and verdict, maybe 100-200 output tokens.

That shared rubric is exactly what <Term w="prompt caching">A provider feature that stores the processed form of a prompt's beginning. Later requests that start with the exact same text reuse it and pay a fraction of the normal input price for that part. Any change to the prefix, even one character, misses the cache.</Term> is for. Put the rubric first, byte-for-byte identical every call, and the item after it.

The numbers in Figure 005, worked out (and checked in Python):

- No cache: 1,900 input tokens × $3/M = $0.0057, plus 150 output × $15/M = $0.00225, so about $0.008 per call, **$7.95 per 1,000**.
- Cached: 400 uncached × $3/M = $0.0012, plus 1,500 cached × $0.30/M = $0.00045, plus the same $0.00225 output, so **$3.90 per 1,000**. Writing the cache the first time costs a bit more than a normal read (about 1.25x on Anthropic's API), which is noise over 1,000 calls.
- Swap test: every pair is judged twice, so double it.

Three practical things about caching. It's a prefix match, so anything that varies (the item, a timestamp, a request ID) must come *after* the cached part. Most providers have a minimum length below which nothing is cached (it varies by model, from a few hundred to a few thousand tokens), so a tiny rubric won't benefit. And the cache expires after a few minutes idle by default, so run judgments in a steady stream, not one every ten minutes. Many providers also offer a batch API at around half price if you can wait hours for results; for a nightly eval, you can.

Notice what's left after caching: output tokens are now more than half the bill. "Reason before verdict" is worth it, but ask for three sentences of reasoning, not three paragraphs.

### When not to use a judge

The most important rubric rule is the one you apply before writing a rubric: **if a program can check it, use the program.**

- Did the agent extract the right price? Compare to the value on the page. Exact.
- Is the output valid JSON matching the schema? Parse it.
- Did the code pass the tests? Run them.
- Is the answer to the arithmetic problem 42? `==`.
- Does the retrieved document contain the answer span from your labelled set? String match, or the ranking metrics from [Ranking metrics and eval sets](/lessons/ranking-metrics/).

A program has kappa 1.0 with itself, costs nothing, and can't be talked into anything by a confident tone. A judge in these places adds cost, noise and bias and removes nothing. Use the judge for the remainder: relevance, faithfulness, helpfulness, "did this answer the actual question". And even there, prefer reference-guided over open-ended whenever you can write down a gold answer.

There's one more case: **don't use a judge as the training reward without measuring it first.** If you optimise a model against a judge (RL from AI feedback, or picking DPO pairs by judge preference), every bias in the judge becomes a direction the model will learn to move in. A judge with length bias produces a model that pads. That's the "pad the losers" toggle in Figure 003, except the model learns to do it on purpose.

```bash
echo ".env" >> .gitignore
```

The wrapper below does three things. It caches every response to disk keyed by the exact request, so rerunning an analysis never pays twice. It tracks spend from the usage numbers the API returns and refuses to go past the budget. And it marks the system prompt (your rubric) as cacheable. This is the Anthropic Python SDK shape; other SDKs differ, so check their docs.

```python
# llm.py
import hashlib, json, os, pathlib
import anthropic

MODEL = os.environ["JUDGE_MODEL"]
PRICE_IN = float(os.environ["PRICE_IN"])
PRICE_OUT = float(os.environ["PRICE_OUT"])
BUDGET = float(os.environ.get("JUDGE_BUDGET_USD", "3"))
CACHE = pathlib.Path(".cache"); CACHE.mkdir(exist_ok=True)

client = anthropic.Anthropic()  # reads ANTHROPIC_API_KEY from the environment

def spent() -> float:
    return sum(json.loads(f.read_text())["cost"] for f in CACHE.glob("*.json"))

def call(system: str, user: str, max_tokens: int = 1024) -> dict:
    key = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps([MODEL, system, user, max_tokens]).encode()).hexdigest()
    f = CACHE / f"{key}.json"
    if f.exists():
        return json.loads(f.read_text())
    if spent() >= BUDGET:
        raise RuntimeError(f"budget ${BUDGET} reached")
    r = client.messages.create(
        model=MODEL,
        max_tokens=max_tokens,
        system=[{"type": "text", "text": system, "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral"}}],
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": user}],
    )
    u = r.usage
    read = u.cache_read_input_tokens or 0
    wrote = u.cache_creation_input_tokens or 0
    cost = (u.input_tokens * PRICE_IN + read * PRICE_IN * 0.1 + wrote * PRICE_IN * 1.25
            + u.output_tokens * PRICE_OUT) / 1e6
    text = "".join(b.text for b in r.content if b.type == "text")
    out = {"text": text, "in": u.input_tokens, "cache_read": read, "cache_write": wrote,
           "out": u.output_tokens, "cost": cost}
    f.write_text(json.dumps(out))
    return out
```

`input_tokens` here counts only the *uncached* part of the prompt; cached tokens show up in the two `cache_*` fields. If `cache_read_input_tokens` stays at 0 across repeated calls, your rubric is either below the model's minimum cacheable length or something in it is changing between calls. Some current models don't accept a `temperature` setting, which is why it isn't here; judges are not fully deterministic anyway, which is one more reason the disk cache matters.

</Task>

**You vs you.** Probably 80-95% on 20 pairs, lower if you included a lot of near-ties. This is the ceiling. Write it next to every judge number.

**v0 on dev.** Probably decent on the easy pairs and noticeably worse on hard pairs and ties, with a confusion matrix whose TIE column is nearly empty: a vague prompt gives the model no permission to say tie, so it almost never does. Kappa well below accuracy, because the easy pairs make luck-level agreement high.

**Position bias.** Current models are much better than the 2023 ones, so the first-slot win rate may be only a few points above 50%. Even so, expect a meaningful number of inconsistent pairs, concentrated on the hard pairs and ties. Inconsistency on the pairs you found hard is partly honest uncertainty. Inconsistency on pairs you found *easy* is bias, and worth reading one by one.

**Length bias.** With generic fluff, a cheap model will probably flip on some fraction of pad-the-loser cases; with keyword-echo fluff, more. The v3 instruction to ignore length should reduce this, but rarely to zero. If the flip rate doesn't move between v2 and v3, the instruction isn't working and you need length control instead: trim both results to the same length before judging.

**Rubric iterations.** Expect the biggest jump from v0 to v1 or v2, mostly from ties being handled, and smaller gains after. Each dev gain of a few points is inside the ±8-10 point interval of 60 items, so trust the direction across several versions more than any single step. If test accuracy is well below your best dev accuracy, you've overfit the rubric to dev, which is exactly why you held test out.

**Cost.** With a cheap model, a 1,500-token rubric and 150 output tokens, expect cents per hundred judgments and roughly a dollar or two per thousand with swapping (it depends heavily on the model's price). Caching should cut the input cost by most of the rubric's share; if your cache-hit count is zero, check the rubric is above the model's minimum cacheable length. The nightly 5,000-query number is the one to take to your team: it's probably a few dollars a night, which is cheap next to a human labelling budget, and that's exactly why it's worth spending one evening making sure it measures the right thing.

## Break It

**1. Let it judge itself.** Generate answers to 30 of your queries (short "what's the best result for this query and why" answers, or summaries of the top result) with two models: the judge's own model and one from a different family. Label which is better yourself. Then measure the judge's win rate for its own family versus your labels. Any gap is self-preference. Now swap in a judge from the other family and see whether the gap flips. This is the reason the judge should never come from the same family as a contestant.

**2. Verdict first.** Change v2 so the verdict comes on the first line and the reasoning after it. Rerun. Accuracy usually drops, and when you read the reasoning on the wrong ones you'll see it confidently justifying whatever letter came first. That's what post-hoc rationalisation looks like, and it's why "explain your answer" placed after the answer is not a check.

**3. Tune on the test set.** Take your best rubric, look at the test-set disagreements, and fix the rubric for them. Test accuracy goes up. Now relabel 30 *fresh* pairs and measure again. It drops back, and probably below your original test number. You've learned nothing about the judge and spent your only clean measurement. This is the same overfitting you'd catch instantly in a model; it's harder to see when the "parameters" are English sentences.

**4. Confident and wrong.** Take 10 pairs where the better result is a plain, hedged answer. Rewrite the *worse* result so it sounds authoritative ("The definitive answer is...", "Experts agree..."), adding no correct information. Count flips. Then add a reference-guided line to the rubric (write a one-line correct answer for each of the 10 queries and give it to the judge) and count again. Reference answers are the defence that doesn't depend on the judge already knowing the truth.

## Explain It Back

Answer out loud, in plain words, as if in an interview. Then check.

<details>
<summary>1. Why use a model as a judge at all, and what's the one number that tells you whether you can trust it?</summary>

Because many outputs worth grading are too open-ended for code (relevance, faithfulness, "did it answer the question") and too numerous for people. A model grades them for fractions of a cent in seconds. But it's standing in for a person, so the number that matters is how often it agrees with a careful person on the same items: agreement with human labels, ideally as Cohen's kappa so luck doesn't inflate it, compared against how often two humans (or you and yourself a day later) agree. Without that number you don't know what the judge is measuring.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What is position bias, how do you measure it, and why does swapping fix it but not length bias?</summary>

The judge favours whichever slot an answer is in, usually the first, independent of content. You measure it by asking each pair in both orders: the consistency rate (same answer wins both times) and the first-slot win rate (should be near 50% if slots were assigned randomly). Swapping fixes it because the push is toward a slot; in the other order it pushes the other answer, so averaging cancels it, exactly so if you average probabilities. Length bias pushes toward a property of an answer, and the longer answer is longer in both orders, so both verdicts are tilted the same way and averaging keeps the tilt. For length you need length control: trim, or regress the length effect out.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Your judge has 88% accuracy against your labels. Your manager says ship the eval. What do you check first?</summary>

The label balance and kappa: if most pairs have the same answer, 88% might be close to luck. The confusion matrix: which kind of mistake makes up the 12%? A judge that never says tie, or always misses one direction, is biased in a way that shifts system comparisons. The confidence interval: on 100 items 88% is roughly ±6 points. Whether 88% was measured on the same items I tuned the rubric on, or on a held-out set. Whether the judge's family matches one of the systems being compared. And the swap test, because one-order accuracy can hide strong position bias that happens to line up with how the pairs were ordered.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. How does the cost of a judge scale, and what do you do when you need to judge 100,000 items a night?</summary>

Cost is items × calls per item × (input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price). The swap test doubles calls. At, say, $4 per 1,000 swapped judgments, 100,000 a night is $400 a night, about $12,000 a month, so it's worth optimising. In order: cache the rubric (it's the same every call, so with caching it costs roughly a tenth); use a batch API if results can wait (typically half price); shorten the reasoning you ask for, since output tokens are the most expensive; use the cheapest model whose kappa against your labels holds up; replace any part of the check a program can do; and judge a random sample (a few thousand items gives a tight enough interval for a win rate) instead of everything. Sampling is usually the biggest win: you rarely need every item judged to compare two systems.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. When would you refuse to use an LLM judge?</summary>

When a program can verify the answer: exact values, schema validity, tests passing, a known answer span. A program is free, exact and unbiased; a judge there only adds noise. Also when I can't get any human labels to calibrate it against, because then I can't know what it measures. And I'd be very careful using it as a training reward without measuring it first, because the model being trained will learn to exploit every bias it has: a length-biased reward produces a model that pads.

</details>

## Go Deeper

- **Lianmin Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena"** (NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks; arXiv 2306.05685). The paper that named and measured the practice. Read the sections on position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias, and the agreement tables.
- **Peiyi Wang et al., "Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators"** (2023, arXiv 2305.17926). Shows order alone can flip benchmark winners, and proposes balanced position calibration, the swap-and-average idea in this lesson.
- **Yann Dubois et al., "Length-Controlled AlpacaEval: A Simple Way to Debias Automatic Evaluators"** (2024, arXiv 2404.04475). How to measure the length effect with a regression and remove it, done on a real leaderboard.
- **Arjun Panickssery, Samuel R. Bowman, Shi Feng, "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations"** (2024). The careful study of self-preference.
- **Paul Thomas, Seth Spielman, Nick Craswell, Bhaskar Mitra, "Large language models can accurately predict searcher preferences"** (SIGIR 2024; arXiv 2309.10621). Bing's experience using LLMs for search relevance labels: how prompt details change agreement with real searchers. Your domain exactly.
- **Eugene Yan, "Evaluating the Effectiveness of LLM-Evaluators (aka LLM-as-Judge)"** (eugeneyan.com, 2024). A long, practical survey of the research, organised by use case, with numbers.
- **Hamel Husain, "Creating a LLM-as-a-Judge That Drives Business Results"** (hamel.dev, 2024). The practitioner's version of this lesson: one domain expert, binary verdicts with critiques, iterate until agreement is high.
- **Jacob Cohen, "A Coefficient of Agreement for Nominal Scales"** (Educational and Psychological Measurement, 1960). Where kappa comes from. Short, and the reasoning about chance agreement is clearer than most textbook versions.
